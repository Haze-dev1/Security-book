# Requirements Gathering and Technical Specifications

:::objectives
- Elicit real requirements from stakeholders who describe solutions, symptoms or wishes rather than needs.
- Distinguish functional requirements, non-functional requirements and constraints, and write each testably.
- Produce a technical specification that a team can build from and a client can agree to.
:::

## Why requirements work is engineering

Most failed projects did not fail at the code. They built the wrong thing, or the right thing for the wrong constraints: a system that worked but could not be operated by the client, met every feature in the list but not the regulatory requirement nobody mentioned, or scaled to a load the business never reached while missing the latency that users actually noticed. Requirements gathering is the discipline of finding out what must be true for the project to succeed, from people who usually cannot tell you directly.

## Stakeholders say solutions; you need problems

A client says "we need a mobile app". Behind that may be "our field staff cannot access records on site" (a problem solvable by a responsive web app in a day, or by offline sync in a quarter). Ask **why** until you reach an outcome someone cares about, then ask **how you would know** it was achieved. The five whys works here as well as in postmortems.

Useful questions:

- Who uses this, how often, from where, on what device, under what pressure?
- What happens today without it? What does that cost?
- What would make this a failure even if every feature were delivered?
- What must never happen? (Data exposed, double charge, lost order, downtime during trading hours.)
- Who else is affected: operations, support, finance, legal, security, the client's clients?
- What existing systems must this talk to, and who owns them?
- What is the budget for building and, separately, for running?
- When must it be live, and what drives that date?
- Who decides when there is a conflict?

Talk to the people who will *use* and *operate* the system, not only the sponsor; observe the current process where possible (a two-hour visit to the warehouse replaces twenty assumptions).

## Classifying what you hear

| Kind | Describes | Example | Must be |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Functional requirement** | What the system does | "A manager can approve an expense claim under 500 EUR without finance review" | Testable with a scenario |
| **Non-functional requirement** | How well it does it | "Approval page loads within 1 s at p95 for 200 concurrent users"; "99.9% monthly availability during 06:00–22:00 CET" | Measurable with a number and a method |
| **Constraint** | What limits the solution space | "Must run in the client's AWS account in eu-central-1"; "Must authenticate via their Entra ID"; "Budget 400 USD/month" | Explicit, with its source |
| **Assumption** | Things taken as true that could be false | "The accounting API supports idempotent invoice creation" | Written down and verified early |
| **Out of scope** | Explicitly excluded | "Mobile native apps"; "Migration of historical data before 2020" | Stated so nobody discovers it later |

Non-functional requirements are the ones nobody volunteers and everyone assumes: availability, latency, data retention, privacy, accessibility, audit, backup, security posture, supported browsers, localisation, operability (who will run it with what skills). Walk a checklist with the stakeholder; the production readiness checklist in Part XV works as a prompt list.

## Writing testable requirements

Weak: "The system should be fast and secure." Strong: "Search results return within 800 ms at p95 under 50 concurrent users with the full 2 million-record dataset" and "All personal data is encrypted at rest with customer-managed keys; access to records is logged with user, time and record ID and retained for 2 years." Each strong requirement implies its verification: a load test, an audit query.

Use **acceptance criteria** in Given/When/Then form for functional behaviour:

```text
Given a manager with a direct report who submitted a claim of 450 EUR
When the manager approves the claim
Then the claim status becomes "approved", finance is not notified, and the submitter receives an email within 5 minutes

Given the same manager and a claim of 500 EUR or more
When the manager approves
Then the claim status becomes "pending finance review" and the finance queue shows it
```

Boundary cases (exactly 500), error cases (approver is the submitter), and permission cases belong in the criteria, because they are where disagreements hide.

## Prioritising

Not everything can be in scope for the date and budget. MoSCoW (must, should, could, won't this time) forces the conversation; "must" means the release is useless without it. Each requirement gets a priority agreed with the decision-maker, and the "won't" list is as valuable as the "must" list. Revisit when the estimate (chapter 2) shows the musts alone exceed the budget.

## The technical specification

A specification turns requirements into an agreed design that a team can estimate and build. Length matches risk: a two-page spec for a small internal tool, thirty pages for a regulated platform. Structure:

```checklist
# Technical specification sections
- Summary: the problem, the proposed solution, the outcome in one paragraph
- Goals and non-goals: measurable goals; explicit exclusions
- Stakeholders and users: who, what they need, who decides
- Requirements: functional (with acceptance criteria), non-functional (with numbers), constraints, assumptions
- Proposed architecture: diagram, components, data flows, trust boundaries; alternatives considered and why rejected
- Data model and APIs: key entities, ownership, retention; external interfaces and contracts
- Security and privacy: threat model summary, authentication and authorisation, data classification, compliance obligations
- Operations: environments, deployment, monitoring, SLOs, backup/DR, on-call model, cost estimate
- Integration: client systems touched, owners, access needed, failure modes
- Delivery plan: phases, milestones, dependencies, risks and mitigations
- Open questions: with owners and dates to resolve
- Approval: who signs off, and what changes require re-approval
```

Write the spec to be read by two audiences: the client's decision-maker, who reads the summary, goals, plan and cost; and the engineers, who read everything. Keep it in version control with the project; update it as decisions change (chapter 3's ADRs record the why).

:::fde The spec is a conversation, not a document
Walk the client through the draft in a meeting; watch where they frown. The non-goals section causes the most useful arguments: "wait, we assumed that was included" is far cheaper to hear now. Get explicit agreement on the non-functional numbers; they drive cost, and a client who agreed to 99.9% in writing will understand why 99.99% later costs more.
:::

## Handling change

Requirements change because understanding improves. The spec and the plan are baselines; a lightweight change process (what changed, why, impact on scope, cost and date, who approved) keeps change visible rather than silent. Scope creep is not malice; it is the absence of this process.

## Common mistakes

- Accepting the stated solution without finding the problem.
- Talking only to the sponsor; the operators and users surprise you at launch.
- Non-functional requirements left implicit ("obviously it must be secure").
- Requirements that cannot be tested ("user-friendly").
- No non-goals, so everything is in scope.
- Specs written once and never updated; the code drifts and the document lies.
- Assuming integration systems work as documented without a spike to verify.

2. Write a two-page technical specification for the small-client portal from Part IX chapter 17 using the section list; have someone else review it for untestable statements.
