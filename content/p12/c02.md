# Scoping and Estimation

:::objectives
- Break work into estimable pieces and estimate with ranges and confidence rather than single numbers.
- Account for the work that is not features: infrastructure, security, testing, operations, documentation, integration, and the unknowns.
- Communicate estimates as commitments with assumptions and buffers, and re-estimate as learning arrives.
:::

:::prereq
[Requirements Gathering and Technical Specifications](#/p12/c01).

## Why estimates are wrong, and still necessary

Software estimates are notoriously inaccurate because the work is discovery: you cannot know how long it takes to integrate with a system you have never seen. Yet clients need budgets and dates to make decisions. The goal is not a precise number but an honest range with stated assumptions that narrows as the work proceeds, plus a plan that tolerates the inevitable surprises.

The **cone of uncertainty**: at the idea stage, actuals range from 0.25× to 4× the estimate; after requirements, 0.5× to 2×; after design, 0.67× to 1.5×. Present estimates with that honesty, and invest in narrowing the cone (spikes, prototypes, discovery) before committing to fixed prices.

## Decompose first

Estimate small things and sum them; estimates of large undifferentiated things are guesses. Work breakdown structure: epics → features → tasks of one to three days each. Anything you cannot break down to that size is something you do not understand yet, which is itself a finding: schedule a **spike** (a time-boxed investigation) to learn enough to estimate.

The decomposition must include the work engineers forget when they imagine "building the feature":

```checklist
# Work that is not the feature
- Environments and infrastructure as code (dev, staging, production, per-client)
- CI/CD pipelines, scanning, artefact management
- Authentication and authorisation integration with the client's identity provider
- Data model, migrations and seed/reference data
- Integration with each external system: discovery, access, contract, error handling, testing with their test environments (often the longest pole)
- Security: threat model, controls, review, penetration test remediation
- Observability: logging, metrics, dashboards, alerts, runbooks
- Testing: unit, integration, end-to-end, load, accessibility, UAT support
- Data migration from the previous system, with validation and rollback
- Documentation: architecture, operations runbooks, user guides, handover
- Deployment to production, go-live support, hypercare period
- Project overhead: meetings, demos, client reviews, change requests
- Compliance and procurement: security questionnaires, DPAs, access approvals, waiting for the client
```

Each of these is typically 5–15% of total effort; together they often exceed the feature work. An estimate that covers only features is half an estimate.

## Estimating techniques

- **Three-point estimates**: for each task, best case (O), most likely (M), worst case (P); expected = (O + 4M + P) / 6; the spread indicates risk. Sum expectations; sum variances for the total range.
- **Reference class**: how long did similar work actually take last time? Your team's historical data beats anyone's intuition; keep records of estimate versus actual.
- **Planning poker / wideband Delphi**: independent estimates from several engineers, discussed where they diverge; divergence reveals hidden assumptions.
- **T-shirt sizing** for early roadmaps (S/M/L/XL with historical day ranges) when detail does not exist yet.
- **Story points and velocity** for ongoing teams; convert to dates only with measured velocity, never with assumed velocity.

```text
Task: integrate invoice sync with the client's accounting API
  O = 3 d (API as documented, sandbox available, idempotent)
  M = 7 d (sandbox requires their IT to provision access; pagination quirks; one undocumented field)
  P = 20 d (no sandbox; rate limits; their vendor must enable an endpoint; a change request on their side)
  Expected = (3 + 28 + 20) / 6 = 8.5 d;  σ ≈ (20 − 3) / 6 ≈ 2.8 d
```

## Dependencies and the critical path

Waiting is work time that nobody books: client access approvals, firewall rules, vendor sandboxes, security reviews, procurement. Identify every external dependency, who owns it, its lead time, and start those requests on day one. Draw the critical path; the project's duration is bounded by the longest chain of dependent tasks, often "get access to the client's systems" rather than any engineering task.

## Buffers, confidence and commitments

Present estimates as ranges with confidence: "10–14 weeks at 80% confidence, assuming the accounting sandbox is available by week 2 and the client's security review completes within two weeks of submission." Buffers are explicit, sized from the variance of the estimates and the number of unknowns, and owned by the project (not hidden in each task, which encourages padding and Parkinson's law). Fixed-price work needs a larger buffer and a tighter scope definition; time-and-materials work needs frequent re-forecasting instead.

Distinguish **estimate** (how long we think it will take), **target** (when the business wants it), and **commitment** (what we promise). Reconcile them by changing scope, resources or date, never by pretending the estimate is smaller. Say explicitly which one a number is.

## Re-estimating

Estimates improve with information. Re-forecast at every milestone with actuals to date, remaining work, and discovered work. A burn-up chart (scope line and completed line over time) shows both progress and scope growth honestly. Report changes early: a client told in week 3 that integration is slipping two weeks can plan; told in week 11, they cannot.

:::fde Fixed price, fixed scope, fixed date: choose two
When a client wants all three fixed, your protection is a precise scope with explicit non-goals, a change process with a price for every change, assumptions written into the contract (access, sandboxes, review turnaround), and a discovery phase sold separately before the fixed price is set. An FDE who gives a fixed price before discovery is gambling with the company's money.
:::

## Common mistakes

- Estimating features only; forgetting the other half of the work.
- Single-number estimates that become commitments in the client's mind.
- Padding every task secretly instead of an explicit, visible buffer.
- Assuming external parties respond immediately.
- Estimating in ideal days and scheduling in calendar days without accounting for meetings, support, leave and interruptions (a 60–70% focus factor is typical).
- Not recording actuals, so the next estimate is no better.
- Letting a target masquerade as an estimate because the client wanted a date.

## Exercises

1. Decompose the small-client portal into tasks of one to three days, including every non-feature category; three-point estimate each; compute the expected total and range.
2. Identify all external dependencies and lead times for that project; draw the critical path and find the real bottleneck.
3. Compare three past estimates with actuals; identify which categories were underestimated and adjust your reference classes.
