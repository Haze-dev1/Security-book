# Client Handovers, Operational Ownership and Professional Incident Management

:::objectives
- Transfer a production system to a client or another team so that it keeps running, patched and understood after you leave.
- Define operational ownership explicitly: who runs what, with what authority, support model and escalation.
- Manage incidents professionally when a client is affected: process, communication, accountability and follow-through.
:::

## Handover is a project phase, not an email

A system handed over badly degrades predictably: nobody patches it, alerts go to departed people, the certificate expires, a secret rotates and nothing updates it, and eighteen months later it is an incident with no one who understands it (the small-client case study). A handover is a planned phase with its own deliverables, acceptance criteria and sign-off, started weeks before the end of the engagement, not on the last day.

## Handover deliverables

Beyond the documentation set in chapter 9:

- **System inventory**: every account, environment, resource group, domain, certificate, third-party service, with owner, cost and renewal date.
- **Credentials and access inventory**: every secret, key, service account and who holds it; rotation procedure and schedule; nothing in your possession that the client does not also control.
- **Risk register**: known weaknesses, technical debt, deferred work, with impact and recommendation; accepted by the client in writing.
- **Cost report**: current monthly spend by component, budget alarms, expected growth.
- **Maintenance calendar**: patching cadence, dependency updates, certificate renewals, restore drills, access reviews, platform end-of-life dates, with the client owner for each.
- **Support model**: who the client calls, for what, with what response times; escalation path; what is in and out of scope.
- **Training record**: who received what training, what they demonstrated.

:::fde Leave nothing that only you can do
If a procedure depends on your laptop, your account, your memory or your relationship with a vendor contact, the handover is incomplete. The test is brutal and simple: could the receiving team recover from a Region failure, a leaked credential and a bad deploy next month without contacting you? Run that test before you sign.
:::

## Operational ownership

Ambiguity about who owns production is how systems rot. Write it down in a **service ownership record** for each system:

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| Service | Name, purpose, criticality tier, SLOs |
| Owning team | Named team; a role, not an individual; escalation manager |
| On-call | Schedule, hours of coverage, response times by severity, tooling |
| Authority | What the owner may do without approval (deploy, roll back, scale, enable degradation, block traffic) and what needs approval (schema changes, cost increases above X, data access) |
| Dependencies | Upstream systems and their owners; downstream consumers |
| Runbooks and dashboards | Links |
| Review cadence | Quarterly ownership review; annual architecture review |

In a client context, the record also states the split between the client and your organisation (shared responsibility, like the cloud model): the client owns their account, identity, data and business decisions; the support provider owns application operations within the contracted hours; the cloud provider owns the infrastructure below the service line. Gaps in this split are where incidents fall through.

Whatever the model, the contract states hours, severity definitions, response and resolution targets, what counts as an incident versus a change request, how emergencies are authorised, and how costs for out-of-scope work are agreed.

## Professional incident management with clients

When a client's production system has an incident, the engineering response (Part XI chapter 6) is half the job; the other half is professional conduct:

- **Notify proactively** within the contracted time (and faster for severe impact), even before you know the cause: "We are aware of errors affecting sign-in since 09:04; we are investigating; next update by 09:45."
- **One voice**: a named communicator for the client; engineers do not improvise updates in client channels.
- **Impact, actions, next update** in every message; no speculation about causes, no blaming vendors or the client, even when accurate, until the postmortem.
- **Involve the client where needed**: they may need to act (their vendor, their DNS, their users); tell them exactly what you need and by when.
- **Record everything** in a shared timeline the client can see.
- **Decisions with impact on their business are theirs**: whether to fail over with potential data loss, whether to disable a feature, whether to notify their customers. Present the options and your recommendation quickly; execute their decision.
- **Preserve evidence** that a regulator or their security team may need.
- **Follow through**: a written incident report within the contracted period (typically 3–5 business days) with timeline, impact, cause, what you did, what you will change, and dates; then deliver those changes and report completion.

The incident report for a client is the postmortem (Part XI chapter 7) written for their audience: blameless, factual, specific about actions, honest about your part. A provider who writes "the outage was caused by our deployment process allowing an untested configuration change; we have added X and Y" earns more trust than one who writes "a configuration issue occurred".

## Security incidents involving client data

Additional obligations: notify the client's security contact immediately under the contract's breach clause (often within 24 hours, sometimes less); support their regulatory notification (GDPR's 72 hours runs from *their* awareness, which your notification triggers); contain in coordination with them; do not communicate externally about their incident without their agreement; preserve forensic evidence; involve your own legal and security leads. Rehearse this path in advance; the contract's notification clause should be in the incident runbook.

## When you are the one inheriting

Receiving a system from another provider or a departing team: run the same checklist in reverse; demand the inventory, credentials, risk register and a knowledge-transfer period; perform the deploy, rollback and restore yourself before accepting; rotate every credential on day one; audit access; baseline monitoring and costs; document what is missing and get it accepted as risk. Do not sign for a system you cannot operate.

## Common mistakes

- Handover compressed into the last week; documentation written from memory.
- Access left with the departing team; alerts routed to them for years.
- "Ownership" assigned to a person who then leaves.
- Support promised at hours the team cannot staff.
- Incident updates that go silent while engineers debug.
- Client incident reports that blame or obscure.
- Accepting an inherited system without operating it first.

## Exercises

1. Build the handover deliverable set for a system you run, using the Part XV checklist; have a colleague unfamiliar with the system perform a deploy, rollback and restore from it; log every failure as a documentation bug.
2. Write the service ownership record and a support model proposal for a client system, including the shared-responsibility split.
3. Draft the client-facing incident report for a past incident, blameless and specific, and compare it with what was actually sent.
