# Secure Development Lifecycle and Compliance Awareness

:::objectives
- Integrate security into every phase of delivery (requirements, design, implementation, verification, release, operations) rather than bolting it on at the end.
- Recognise the major compliance frameworks (GDPR, SOC 2, ISO 27001, PCI DSS, HIPAA) well enough to know what they demand of engineering and when to involve specialists.
- Produce the evidence auditors need as a by-product of normal engineering work.
:::

## Security as a lifecycle property

A penetration test the week before launch finds problems that cost ten times more to fix than if found at design. A **secure development lifecycle (SDL)** distributes security activities across the phases where they are cheapest:

The activities scale with risk: a public payment system gets a formal threat model and an external penetration test; an internal dashboard gets the checklist and the scanners. Define risk tiers and the mandatory activities per tier so the decision is not made ad hoc.

## Security requirements and abuse cases

Alongside user stories, write **abuse cases**: "As an attacker, I want to enumerate other tenants' invoices by changing the ID" with the mitigation ("resource ownership check; 404 for others; rate limiting; alert on enumeration patterns"). They become test cases. Data classification (public, internal, confidential, restricted) decided at requirements time drives every later control: encryption, access, logging, retention, where it may be processed.

## Threat modelling in practice

Thirty minutes per significant design, repeated when architecture changes: draw the data flow diagram with trust boundaries; for each element and boundary crossing, ask STRIDE (spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service, elevation of privilege); record threats, mitigations and accepted risks in the design document. Part IV chapter 2 has the method; the point here is that it is a scheduled lifecycle activity with an output that reviewers check, not an optional exercise.

## Vulnerability management

Findings from scanners, penetration tests, bug reports and advisories need a single process: triage (real? exploitable? severity by CVSS adjusted for context), SLAs by severity (critical: fix or mitigate within days; high: two weeks; medium: a quarter; low: backlog), ownership, tracking, verification of fixes, and exception handling with expiry. Publish the SLAs; measure adherence; report to leadership. A **security.txt** and a disclosure policy let external researchers report responsibly.

## Compliance frameworks: what engineering needs to know

Compliance specialists own the frameworks; engineers must know enough to recognise obligations and build the evidence in.

What they share: know what data you have and where (data map); control who can access what and review it (IAM, SSO, MFA, offboarding, quarterly access reviews); change only through reviewed, approved, logged processes (your pipeline); detect and respond to incidents (monitoring, runbooks, notification timelines); back up and prove recovery; manage vendors (who processes your data, under what agreement); write policies and follow them. The engineering practices in this handbook *are* most of the controls; compliance work is largely about producing evidence that they happen.

## Evidence by design

Auditors ask for proof over a period: "show me that every production change in Q2 was approved by someone other than the author". A team with the Part X pipeline answers with a query over deployment records; a team without it spends weeks assembling screenshots. Design evidence capture into systems: deployment and approval records retained immutably; access review reports generated from IAM and the identity provider; alert and incident records exported; backup restore test reports; vulnerability SLA dashboards; policy acknowledgements tracked. Compliance automation platforms (Vanta, Drata, Secureframe) collect much of this through integrations; they still depend on the underlying controls existing.

## Privacy engineering essentials

Beyond legal text, concrete practices: collect only what the feature needs; separate identifiers from content where possible (pseudonymisation); encrypt personal data with keys you can destroy per tenant or per user (crypto-shredding simplifies deletion); design deletion and export flows across every store, including backups (document the backup retention as the deletion horizon) and third parties; log access to personal data; default to the shortest retention that meets the purpose; involve the data protection officer early for new processing; and make the data map a living artefact updated by the architecture review ([Data Privacy and Personally Identifiable Information](#/p04/c23)).

:::fde Compliance questionnaires
Clients will send security questionnaires (hundreds of questions) before signing. Maintain a current answer bank with evidence links, a security whitepaper, an architecture diagram, pen-test summary letters and certifications. Answer honestly: a "no, but planned by Q2" with a roadmap beats a "yes" that an audit disproves. Know which of your answers depend on the client's own configuration (shared responsibility) and say so.
:::

## Common mistakes

- Security as a phase at the end ("the pen test will find it").
- Threat models written once and never revisited.
- Treating compliance as paperwork disconnected from engineering, so evidence is manufactured at audit time.
- Personal data in logs, test fixtures and analytics without classification.
- Vulnerability findings without SLAs or owners.
- Claiming compliance a team cannot evidence.

## Exercises

1. Define risk tiers for your organisation's systems and the mandatory security activities per tier per phase.
2. Write five abuse cases for a feature with their mitigations; implement them as tests.
3. For one framework your clients care about, map each engineering-relevant control to the practice and the evidence source in your delivery system; identify gaps.
