# Documentation and Stakeholder Communication

:::objectives
- Write the documentation production systems need (architecture, operations, onboarding, API, decisions) at the right level and keep it alive.
- Communicate with technical and non-technical stakeholders clearly: status, risk, trade-offs, bad news.
- Run meetings, write updates and handle escalations so that engineering work is understood and trusted.
:::

[Software Development Lifecycle and Engineering Documentation](#/p01/c12); [Design Documents, Architecture Reviews and ADRs](#/p12/c03).

## Documentation that earns its upkeep

Documentation is read by people under pressure: the on-call engineer at 03:00, the new hire in week one, the client's team after you leave, the auditor. Write for those moments. Four kinds, each with a different job (the Diátaxis model is a useful map): **tutorials** (learning by doing), **how-to guides** (steps for a task), **reference** (precise facts: API, configuration), and **explanation** (why things are as they are). Operations needs how-to and reference; onboarding needs tutorials and explanation; decisions need ADRs.

What every production system should have, in the repository, versioned with the code:

```checklist
# Minimum documentation set
- README: what it is, how to run it locally, how to run tests, where everything else is (one page)
- Architecture overview: diagram with trust boundaries, components, data flows, dependencies, and links to ADRs
- Operations runbook: deploy, roll back, scale, rotate secrets, restore, respond to each alert; environment inventory; access procedure
- Configuration reference: every variable and flag, its meaning, default, and who may change it
- API reference: generated from the OpenAPI specification; authentication; errors; versioning; rate limits
- Data documentation: data map (what personal or sensitive data lives where), retention, ownership
- On-call guide: schedule, escalation, tools, dashboards, severity definitions
- ADRs: significant decisions with context
- Changelog or release notes: what changed when, for humans
- Onboarding guide: how a new engineer becomes productive in a week
```

Keep it alive: documentation changes ship in the same pull request as the code change (reviewers ask "is the runbook updated?"); docs are linted (broken links, stale dates); each document names an owner and a review date; game days and onboarding sessions are the test, and every confusion found is a documentation bug. Delete or archive what is wrong; wrong documentation is worse than none.

Writing style for operational documents: imperative, specific, with the exact command; expected output shown; the failure branches ("if this returns X, do Y"); no assumptions about what the reader knows about the system; a last-verified date.

## Diagrams

A diagram is for one question: structure (components and dependencies), flow (a request's path), deployment (what runs where), or data (what is stored where). Label edges with protocols and directions; draw trust boundaries; show versions and Regions when they matter; prefer diagrams-as-code (Mermaid, Structurizr, PlantUML) kept in the repository so they are reviewable and do not rot in a design tool nobody can open. The C4 model's levels (context, container, component) stop diagrams from trying to show everything at once.

## Communicating with stakeholders

Engineers communicate badly in predictable ways: too much detail, too late, hedged into meaninglessness, or confident without basis. Principles that fix most of it:

- **Lead with what they need to decide or know**; detail follows for those who want it.
- **Translate to impact**: not "the replication lag alarm fired" but "customers may have seen stale balances for 12 minutes; no data was lost; we are fixing the cause."
- **Numbers and dates, with confidence**: "likely 3 weeks, 80% confident, assuming X" beats "soon" and beats a falsely precise "17 days".
- **Bad news early**: a risk reported when it is a risk can be managed; reported when it is an outage, it is a failure of trust as well as of the system.
- **No surprises**: stakeholders should never learn about a problem from someone else first.
- **Options, not just problems**: present two or three choices with trade-offs and a recommendation; let them decide what is theirs to decide.
- **Say what you do not know** and when you will know it.

## Status reporting

A weekly written update that a busy sponsor reads in two minutes:

```markdown
## Portal project — week 7 of 12

**Overall: amber.** On track for the 15 Dec go-live if the accounting sandbox is available by Friday (see risk 1).

**Done this week**: checkout flow complete and tested; staging environment live; security scan clean.
**Next week**: invoice sync integration; load test; begin UAT scripts with finance.

**Risks and decisions needed**
1. Accounting sandbox still not provisioned (requested 3 Nov). Each week of delay moves go-live a week. **Need**: escalation to vendor by client IT.
2. Finance wants multi-currency, not in scope. Estimated +2 weeks. **Decision**: defer to phase 2 (recommended) or extend date.

**Metrics**: 41/58 stories done; 0 open high-severity findings; budget 56% used.
```

Red/amber/green with the reason; done/next/risks; decisions needed with a recommendation; a few metrics. Same format every week so readers know where to look.

## Meetings

Have fewer, make them count: an agenda with decisions sought; the right people only; pre-read sent a day before; a facilitator who keeps time; decisions and actions written in the meeting and circulated within the hour; no meeting for what an update could do. Technical reviews work best asynchronously with a short synchronous resolution. Demos beat slides: show the working software on the real environment.

## Explaining technical matters to non-technical people

Start from their world (what they do, what they fear, what they are measured on), use analogies anchored in it, and quantify: "Multi-AZ is like having a second office with the same files and staff ready; if the first floods, work continues within a minute. It costs 35 USD a month." Avoid jargon or define it once; check understanding by asking them to restate the decision; never make someone feel stupid for asking. Part XIII chapter 14 goes deeper on trade-off conversations.

## Difficult conversations

Scope cuts, missed dates, incidents, security findings in the client's own systems, a decision you think is wrong: prepare the facts and the options; state the situation plainly in the first sentence; own your part without theatrical apology; propose a path; listen; agree next steps in writing. Deliver bad news in person or by voice, then confirm in writing. Escalate through the agreed path when a decision is above your authority, and tell the person you are escalating.

## Writing for people who were not there

Postmortems, handover documents and design documents are read by people months later with no context. Write the context in; define terms; link to evidence; date everything; avoid references to "the usual approach" or "as discussed". The test: could a competent engineer who joined last week act on this document alone?

## Common mistakes

- Documentation in a wiki nobody updates, divergent from the code.
- Runbooks that assume the author's knowledge ("restart the thing").
- Status reports that are activity lists without risks or decisions.
- Hiding risks until they are certainties.
- Explaining technology instead of consequences.
- Meetings as the default medium for everything.

## Exercises

1. Audit a system's documentation against the minimum set; fix the three most dangerous gaps (usually runbook, configuration reference and data map).
2. Rewrite a recent technical status message for a non-technical sponsor using the weekly update format; have a non-engineer tell you what they understood.
3. Practise a difficult conversation (a two-week slip) with a colleague playing the client; prepare facts, options and a recommendation; debrief on what landed.
