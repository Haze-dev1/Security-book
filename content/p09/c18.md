# Case Study: Highly Available Enterprise Application

:::objectives
- Design an application for an enterprise with strict availability (99.99%), compliance and change-control requirements: multi-Region, zero-downtime deployments, audited operations.
- Understand what each extra "nine" costs in architecture, process and money, and how to avoid buying nines that are not needed.
- Integrate the enterprise realities: existing identity, network connectivity, security review boards, and operations teams who will run what you build.
:::

## The brief

A financial-services enterprise needs a customer-facing account servicing application (balances, statements, payments initiation, document centre) used by 3 million customers, with internal staff tooling. Regulatory requirements: 99.99% availability for core functions measured monthly, RPO of 1 minute and RTO of 15 minutes for Region loss, data residency within one country, full audit trail of every data change and staff action, change management with approvals and evidence, penetration testing and third-party risk assessments, encryption with customer-managed keys, and demonstrable disaster recovery tests twice a year. The enterprise already has: Entra ID for staff, a core banking system reachable only over private connectivity, a SOC that ingests logs into a SIEM, a change advisory board, and a cloud platform team with landing zones, guardrails and an approved service list.

## Requirements, quantified

| Area | Requirement | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Availability | 99.99% monthly ≈ 4.3 minutes downtime | No single-Region design; no deployment may cause downtime; every dependency must be HA or degradable |
| RPO / RTO | 1 min / 15 min for Region failure | Synchronous or near-synchronous cross-Region replication; automated failover with regular rehearsal |
| Latency | p99 < 800 ms for account views, including core banking calls | Aggressive caching of reference data; async where possible; the core banking link is the latency floor |
| Scale | 3M customers, 400k daily actives, 2,000 req/s peak, month-end 3× | Moderate scale; the difficulty is availability and compliance, not throughput |
| Compliance | Residency, audit, key management, change evidence | In-country Regions only; append-only audit pipeline; CMKs; CI/CD that produces evidence |
| Operations | Run by the enterprise's operations team | Runbooks, dashboards, alerts and automation designed for them and reviewed with them |

## Key decisions

**Active-active for compute, single-writer for the relational database**: both Regions serve traffic constantly, which proves the standby works and halves the per-Region load; the relational source of truth has one writer with sub-second replication and managed failover, because multi-writer relational databases introduce conflict semantics the domain (money) cannot tolerate. Write-forwarding from Region B adds ~20–40 ms to writes originating there, which the budget allows. Data that fits last-writer-wins (sessions, idempotency records with conditional writes) uses multi-active DynamoDB Global Tables so that a Region failover does not log everyone out.

**Failover is automated, rehearsed and boring**: health checks at DNS and CDN; a runbook-as-code that promotes the Aurora replica, flips DNS weights and raises a banner, executed by a tested workflow with a human approving the trigger (false positives are costlier than 60 seconds of delay); the "unplanned Region loss" drill runs twice a year in production during a low-traffic window with regulators informed, and the "planned Region evacuation" runs quarterly. RTO measured each time and reported.

**Zero-downtime everything**: blue-green or canary deployments per Region with automatic rollback on SLO regression (Part X chapter 7), Regions deployed one at a time with soak time between; expand/contract database migrations only, with a lint that rejects locking DDL; feature flags for behavioural changes; connection draining on every layer; no maintenance windows in the availability budget.

**Dependencies are HA or degradable**: the core banking link is the weakest component (on-premises, change-frozen). A façade service caches balances and statements with explicit freshness labels ("as of 09:42"), queues payment initiations durably when the link is down (customers see "scheduled, will be submitted"), and reconciles. The external IdPs are themselves multi-Region, and token validation is local. Email and notifications are asynchronous and never on the critical path. Every dependency has a documented degradation mode agreed with the business.

**Compliance built into the pipeline**: every change is a pull request with required reviews, automated tests, security scans, infrastructure plan review and a change record created automatically with the evidence (who, what, approvals, test results, deployment logs) attached, satisfying the change advisory board without manual paperwork for standard changes; emergency changes follow a documented, post-reviewed path. Infrastructure drift detection alarms; the approved-service list is enforced by the platform team's service control policies.

**Keys, audit, residency**: customer-managed multi-Region KMS keys with separation of duties for key administration; field-level encryption for the most sensitive attributes; application audit events (who viewed or changed what, when, from where) emitted via the outbox into an append-only store with hash chaining and shipped to the SIEM within a minute; retention per regulation; all Regions in-country; CloudFront configured with price class and geo restrictions as required and reviewed for any edge-caching of personal data (none permitted).

**Staff tooling**: separate deployment, reachable only from the corporate network or a zero-trust access proxy, Entra ID SSO with phishing-resistant MFA, fine-grained RBAC with four-eyes approval for sensitive operations (payment release, customer contact changes), every action audited and reviewable by the compliance team.

**Capacity and resilience testing**: each Region load-tested to 100% of peak plus month-end factor; chaos experiments (AZ loss, dependency latency injection, database failover) in staging monthly and in production in controlled form; game days with the operations team.

The honest conversation with an enterprise is about which functions need 99.99%. Here, balances and payments do; the document centre and statement downloads tolerate 99.9% and are built more simply within the same platform, with their own SLOs.

## Bottlenecks and failure modes

- **Split brain after a hasty failover**: promoting Region B while Region A still accepts writes creates divergent data. The failover workflow fences the old writer first (revoke write access, stop the application's write path) and only then promotes; Aurora's managed failover handles the database side, the workflow handles the application side.
- **Replication lag spikes**: a bulk job in Region A pushes lag to seconds, breaking read-your-writes for Region B users. Route a user's session to the writer Region for a short window after they write, or read from the writer for sensitive confirmations; throttle bulk jobs.
- **Direct Connect loss**: both circuits share a provider segment nobody knew about. Diversity verified with the provider, VPN backup tested quarterly, and the façade's degradation mode exercised.
- **Correlated deploys**: a bad release deployed to both Regions within minutes defeats the redundancy. Mandatory soak between Regions with automatic halt on SLO regression.
- **Control-plane dependencies**: failover scripts that call APIs in the failing Region or depend on a global service in a degraded state. Keep failover tooling Region-independent and tested from both sides.
- **Certificate and DNS mistakes**: the most mundane cause of enterprise outages. Automated renewal, expiry alarms at 30 and 7 days, DNS changes through the pipeline with review.
- **Month-end capacity**: 3× traffic with batch jobs running; scheduled scaling ahead of time, batch windows separated from peak, and tests at month-end volumes.

## Operations model

The enterprise's operations team runs the platform; your team builds it with them, not for them. Deliverables: SLO definitions and dashboards per function, alert catalogue with severity and runbook links, on-call rotation design (follow-the-sun if available), incident process integration (their tooling, their bridge), DR runbooks and drill schedule, capacity reports, security operations integration (SIEM content, GuardDuty findings routing, vulnerability management SLAs), and a quarterly architecture review. Success is the operations team executing a Region failover drill without your team on the call.
