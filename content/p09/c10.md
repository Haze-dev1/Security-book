# Disaster Recovery, Multi-Region Architecture and Cost versus Complexity

:::objectives
- Choose a disaster recovery strategy (backup/restore, pilot light, warm standby, active-active) from RPO/RTO and budget.
- Design multi-Region systems: data replication, traffic routing, failover and failback, and the data consistency consequences.
- Account honestly for the cost and operational complexity of each tier, and recommend proportionately.
- Rehearse disaster recovery so it works when needed.
:::

:::prereq
Chapters 2, 5 and 6; Part V chapter 11; Part VII chapters 1, 9 and 11.
:::

## What counts as a disaster

Beyond the failures handled within a Region (instance, AZ, dependency): loss or prolonged impairment of a whole Region; destruction or corruption of data (malicious or accidental) that replication faithfully propagated; loss of the account or its credentials; a provider-level incident affecting the control plane. DR is the plan for these: proportionate to their likelihood and to what the business loses per hour and per lost transaction.

Most client systems are correctly served by **backup and restore with cross-Region copies and quarterly drills**, upgraded to **pilot light** where an hour of downtime has a clear business cost. Warm standby and active-active are for revenue-critical systems with teams to operate them. Recommend the tier with its cost and the business loss it prevents; let the client choose with the numbers in front of them.

## Pilot light on AWS, concretely

- **Data**: RDS cross-Region read replica (promote on failover; async: minutes of RPO) or Aurora Global Database (sub-second lag, managed failover); DynamoDB Global Tables (active-active by nature); S3 Cross-Region Replication (new objects; Batch Replication for existing); Secrets Manager replication; ECR replication; backups copied by AWS Backup with Vault Lock.
- **Compute**: the same Terraform/CloudFormation applied to the second Region with minimal sizes (ECS service desired count 0 or 1; ASG min 0), images already replicated, parameters and secrets present.
- **Routing**: Route 53 failover records with health checks on the primary, low TTL (60 s), or Global Accelerator for static anycast IPs and faster traffic shifting.
- **Failover runbook**: declare; promote the replica (or Aurora failover); scale compute; update any Region-specific configuration (endpoints, KMS keys: use multi-Region keys); flip DNS or confirm health-check failover; verify; communicate. Time it in drills; RTO is what the drill measures, not what the slide says.
- **Failback**: after the primary Region recovers, replicate back (the former primary is now stale), rehearse the reverse switch, and schedule it in a window. Failback is harder than failover and often skipped in planning.

## Multi-Region active-active

The complexity lives in data. Options:

- **Partition by tenant or geography**: each tenant's data has a home Region; the other Region serves only on failover (for that tenant). Avoids write conflicts; routing must send tenants to their home; cross-tenant features become cross-Region.
- **Globally replicated data stores with conflict resolution**: DynamoDB Global Tables (last-writer-wins by timestamp: design keys and writes to avoid conflicts), Aurora Global Database with write forwarding (writes go to the primary Region; reads local), CRDT-based stores for mergeable data.
- **Read-local, write-global**: reads served from the nearest Region's replica; writes routed to a single primary Region (latency for writes; strong consistency preserved). Often the pragmatic compromise.

Everything else multiplies: deployments to multiple Regions (pipelines, staged rollouts per Region), observability aggregated globally, secrets and keys per Region, compliance (data residency may forbid replication to some Regions), cost of cross-Region data transfer, and testing of failure modes between Regions (partial partitions). A single-Region multi-AZ design plus pilot light achieves 99.9%+ for most purposes; active-active is for 99.99% and beyond with an organisation to match (chapter 2).

## Protecting against data destruction

Replication is not protection against `DROP TABLE`, ransomware encryption, or a bug writing bad data; it replicates the damage. DR for data mistakes is **point-in-time recovery and immutable backups** (Part V chapter 11; Part VIII chapter 9): PITR windows long enough to discover the mistake, backup copies in a separate account with Vault Lock that production credentials cannot delete, and a rehearsed procedure to restore to a point before the damage and reconcile. Include "malicious insider with admin rights" and "compromised CI pipeline" in the scenarios.

## Account and credential loss

Losing control of the AWS account (root compromise, billing suspension) is a disaster class of its own. Mitigations: root MFA and locked credentials, Organizations with SCPs preventing member accounts from leaving or disabling logging, backups in a *separate* account, IaC and runbooks in a repository outside the account, and the ability to rebuild in a fresh account from code plus backups (which the quarterly rebuild drill proves).

## Drills

A DR plan that has not been exercised is fiction. Cadence: monthly automated restore tests (Part XI chapter 9); quarterly tabletop walk-through of the regional failure runbook with everyone named in it; at least annually a real failover to the DR Region in a maintenance window (or a full rebuild in a sandbox account from code and backups), timed, with findings turned into fixes. Report drill results to the client as evidence; they are also the honest measurement of RTO.

## Cost versus complexity: the conversation

:::fde
Frame DR for a client as a purchase: "An hour of downtime costs you roughly X in lost orders and Y in reputation; this tier costs Z per month and recovers in about N minutes with M minutes of data loss; the next tier costs 2Z and recovers in a few minutes with near-zero loss but needs a team to run it. Here is what I recommend and why." Write the chosen tier, its RPO/RTO, its cost and its drill schedule into the DR plan and the readiness review. Revisit annually or when the business changes.
:::

## Common mistakes

- "We have Multi-AZ, so we are covered" (Region loss and data destruction are not covered).
- DR plan that depends on the IaC being complete, never tested by a full rebuild.
- Backups in the same account as production.
- Failover rehearsed, failback never considered.
- Active-active chosen for prestige; conflict bugs and double costs for a system that needed pilot light.
- KMS keys, secrets, ECR images or Route 53 configuration missing in the DR Region; discovered during the incident.
- Health-check-based DNS failover with TTL 300 and clients that cache longer; 15 minutes of outage after a 2-minute failover.
- Drills skipped because "we are busy".

## Exercises

1. Write the DR plan for a system: scenarios, tier, RPO/RTO, runbooks for failover and failback, drill schedule, and the cost of the tier versus the next one up.
2. Implement pilot light in a sandbox (cross-Region RDS replica, replicated images and secrets, zero-scaled compute, failover DNS); run a failover and failback; time both.
3. Perform a full rebuild of an environment in a fresh sandbox account from IaC and backups alone; list every missing piece.
