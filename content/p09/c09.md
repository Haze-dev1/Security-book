# Service Discovery, Fault Tolerance, Graceful Degradation and Rate Limiting

:::objectives
- Choose a service discovery mechanism (DNS, load balancers, registries, meshes) and understand its failure modes.
- Design services that tolerate dependency failure through degradation levels and kill switches.
- Apply rate limiting and load shedding at the right layers to protect capacity and fairness.
- Put the pieces together into a service that stays useful when parts of its world are broken.
:::

## Service discovery

Services need to find each other's current addresses as instances come and go.

Failure modes to design for: stale DNS caches (set client TTL handling; JVMs historically cached forever), registries down (clients should keep using the last known good list), health checks lying (chapter: Part XI chapter 5), and long-lived connections pinned to removed instances (rebalance; chapter 3).

## Fault tolerance through degradation levels

Define, per service, what it does when each dependency is unavailable, as explicit **degradation levels** rather than ad-hoc exception handling:

| Dependency down | Level | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Recommendation service | Minor | Hide recommendations; log; no user-visible error |
| Search index | Moderate | Fall back to database `ILIKE` with a result limit and a "limited search" notice |
| Payment provider | Major | Accept orders in `payment_pending`; queue charges; notify when processed; block if the queue exceeds a bound |
| Session store | Critical | Fail closed: 503 for authenticated routes; static pages still served |
| Primary database | Critical | 503 with `Retry-After`; read-only mode from replica if designed |

Each level is implemented with a timeout, a circuit breaker and a fallback (chapter 8), a **kill switch** feature flag to force the level manually during incidents (Part X chapter 7), and a metric showing which level is active. The product owner agrees the levels in advance; during an incident nobody debates whether hiding recommendations is acceptable.

## Rate limiting, revisited at system level

Part III chapter 5 built limiters. At the architecture level, rate limiting is layered:

1. **Edge** (WAF rate rules, CDN): blunt per-IP limits to absorb floods and bots before they cost compute.
2. **Gateway / ingress**: per-API-key or per-tenant limits enforcing plans and fairness; quotas.
3. **Service**: per-route and per-tenant limits for expensive operations; concurrency limits (bulkheads) protecting downstream dependencies.
4. **Client-side**: your own calls to third parties throttled to their quotas; retry with backoff on 429.
5. **Database**: statement timeouts and connection limits as the last line.

Limits must be visible (headers, metrics), documented, and fail safe (if the limiter's store is down, decide: allow with a local fallback limiter, or deny; for authentication endpoints prefer deny).

## Load shedding and prioritisation

When demand exceeds capacity, choose who to disappoint. Mechanisms: **admission control** by in-flight request count or queue length (reject at the door with 503 rather than queue into timeouts); **priority classes** (health checks and authenticated checkout above anonymous browsing above analytics ingestion); **tenant fairness** (per-tenant concurrency so one tenant's burst does not starve others); **shedding non-essential work** first (disable background recomputation, lower sampling, defer notifications); **autoscaling** as the medium-term relief, with shedding covering the minutes it takes. Shedding is designed and tested in advance; improvised shedding during an incident means random failures.

## Testing fault tolerance

For each dependency: inject failure (down, slow, erroring) in staging and verify the degradation level engages, metrics show it, the SLO holds for the core path, and recovery is automatic when the dependency returns (breaker half-open works; queues drain). Run as game days (Part XI chapter 9) and automate the most important as chaos experiments in staging.

## Common mistakes

- Discovery via hard-coded IPs or hostnames of individual instances.
- Degradation improvised during incidents; no agreed levels; no kill switches.
- Treating every dependency as critical (one down equals all down) or none as critical (serving wrong answers).
- Rate limits only at one layer; floods reach the application, or legitimate tenants starve each other.
- Shedding that drops health checks or paying customers first.
- Fault injection never performed; the first test is production.

## Exercises

1. Write the degradation table for a service: every dependency, its level, behaviour, fallback and kill switch; implement two of the fallbacks.
2. Inject failures (down, slow) for each dependency in staging and record whether the agreed level engaged and the SLO held.
3. Implement admission control with priority classes in a service and load test past capacity; confirm health checks and the critical path keep succeeding while low-priority traffic is shed.
