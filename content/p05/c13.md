# Infrastructure Troubleshooting and Common Incidents

:::objectives
- Apply a repeatable diagnostic method to any server problem, from symptom to root cause, without guessing.
- Recognise the incidents every operator eventually meets and resolve them from a mental catalogue.
- Preserve evidence and communicate while troubleshooting.
- Turn each incident into a prevention measure.
:::

:::prereq
All previous chapters in this part; Part II chapter 19.
:::

## The problem

Under pressure, engineers reach for the first plausible action: restart the service, reboot the server, scale up. Sometimes it works and the cause is never learned, so it returns. A method replaces guessing with a sequence that finds the cause quickly and leaves evidence. The common incidents below are the ones that method keeps finding; knowing them in advance halves the time to recognise them.

## The catalogue of common incidents

Each entry: how it presents, how to confirm, how to fix, how to prevent. Several appeared as full scenarios in earlier chapters; they are listed here so the catalogue is complete.

### Disk full

Presents as: writes failing everywhere at once (database errors, log errors, "no space left on device", services failing to start), often preceded by nothing. Confirm: `df -h`, `df -i`. Fix: find the consumer (`du`, `docker system df`, `journalctl --disk-usage`, `lsof +L1` for deleted-open files), remove or move; for databases, free space before anything else or risk corruption. Prevent: separate volumes, 80% alerts, rotation, pruning timers (chapters 6, 9; Part I chapter 4 scenario).

### Out of memory

Presents as: processes disappearing, exit code 137, `Killed process` in `dmesg`, swap thrashing with everything slow. Confirm: `dmesg -T | grep -i oom`, `free`, per-process RSS. Fix: restart the offender to recover; cap it (systemd `MemoryMax`, container limits); add memory if legitimately needed. Prevent: memory alarms, limits, leak detection (Part I chapter 7).

### CPU saturation and burst credit exhaustion

Presents as: everything slow, load average high, `%st` high on burstable instances. Confirm: `top`, `vmstat`, CloudWatch `CPUCreditBalance`. Fix: find the hot process (`top`, `pidstat`), kill runaway jobs, scale up or out, change instance family. Prevent: credit alarms, non-burstable types for sustained load, autoscaling (chapters 1, 10).

### Service down or crash-looping

Presents as: 502/503 at the proxy, `systemctl status` showing `failed` or `activating (auto-restart)`. Confirm: `journalctl -u svc -n 100`. Causes: bad config (validation failure), missing dependency (database not reachable), port in use, permission denied, missing file after deploy, out of file descriptors. Fix per cause; `reset-failed` after start-limit. Prevent: preflight checks, config validation, alerts on `--failed` (chapter 5 scenario).

### Certificate expired

Presents as: all HTTPS clients failing at once, often at a round hour; browsers show certificate errors; API clients fail TLS. Confirm: `openssl s_client ... | openssl x509 -noout -dates`. Fix: renew now (Caddy restart, `certbot renew --force-renewal` or DNS-01 fallback) and reload the proxy. Prevent: automated renewal with hooks, external expiry monitoring (chapter 8; Part II chapter 10 scenario).

### DNS misconfiguration

Presents as: "cannot reach" from some users or all, with servers healthy; NXDOMAIN or stale IPs. Confirm: `dig +trace`, compare resolvers. Fix: correct the record; wait out TTL; in emergencies, communicate the IP directly only as a stopgap. Prevent: `dig +trace` after every change; low TTLs around migrations; delegation checks (Part II chapter 7 scenario).

### Connectivity blocked (firewall, security group, route)

Presents as: timeouts to a specific destination; works from one host, not another. Confirm: `nc -vz -w3`, `tcpdump` on the target, Reachability Analyzer, flow logs. Fix: the offending rule or route. Prevent: infrastructure as code for network rules, change review (Part II chapters 15, 19).

### Database connection exhaustion

Presents as: `FATAL: too many connections` / `remaining connection slots are reserved`, or pool timeouts in the application; often after scaling out the app tier. Confirm: `SELECT count(*) FROM pg_stat_activity;`, per-application pool settings × instances. Fix: reduce pool sizes, terminate idle connections (`pg_terminate_backend` for idle-in-transaction), add PgBouncer/RDS Proxy. Prevent: compute total connections before scaling; pooler; `idle_in_transaction_session_timeout` (Part VIII chapter 6).

### Clock drift

Presents as: TLS errors on some hosts, AWS API `SignatureDoesNotMatch`/`RequestTimeTooSkewed`, JWTs rejected, log timelines that do not line up. Confirm: `timedatectl`, `chronyc tracking`. Fix: restart chrony, step the clock (`chronyc makestep`). Prevent: chrony everywhere, alarm on offset (Part I chapter 3).

### Deploy went wrong

Presents as: errors starting exactly at deploy time. Confirm: deploy log, version endpoint, compare with the previous release. Fix: roll back (previous image/release symlink/AMI), then investigate. Prevent: staging, canaries, automated rollback triggers (Part X).

### Runaway log or job

Presents as: disk, CPU or database load climbing steadily; a timer or cron job that began failing and retrying, or logging in a loop. Confirm: `systemctl list-timers`, `journalctl -u job`, per-process I/O. Fix: stop the job; clean up. Prevent: job alerting on failure *and* on duration, rate limits on logging.

### Stale or missing secret

Presents as: authentication failures to a dependency after a rotation or a redeploy; `401`/`403` from a provider, database password rejected. Confirm: when was the secret rotated; which version does the service hold (started before rotation?). Fix: restart to pick up the new value, or fix the reference. Prevent: rotation procedures that include restarts or runtime refresh; alerts on auth failures to dependencies (Part IV chapter 17).

### Noisy neighbour / throttling

Presents as: intermittent slowness with no change in your metrics; EBS or network throttling counters rising. Confirm: ENA `*_allowance_exceeded`, EBS `BurstBalance`, `%st`. Fix: larger instance or volume class. Prevent: size to allowances; alarms on throttling metrics (chapter 10).

### Reboot that did not come back

Presents as: instance unreachable after a reboot or kernel update. Confirm: serial console / system log screenshot. Causes: `fstab`, broken bootloader, service blocking boot, sshd config. Fix: rescue volume procedure (chapter 2). Prevent: `nofail`, `sshd -t`, test reboots in staging.

## Evidence and communication

- Before changing anything: copy the relevant logs (`journalctl -u svc --since ... > /tmp/incident-svc.log`), snapshot the volume if data may be corrupted, record `ps`, `ss`, `df`, `free` output, note the time.
- During: post each action and its result in the incident channel with timestamps. Say what you are about to do before you do it; someone may know why not.
- Status updates to the client on a fixed cadence (every 30 minutes for a Sev1), even if the update is "still investigating, next update at HH:MM" (Part XI chapter 6).
- After: the timeline is the postmortem's skeleton; good notes make it a 30-minute job.

## Habits that make troubleshooting fast

- Know your normal: baseline dashboards for every resource so abnormal is visible at a glance.
- Keep the toolkit installed on images (`htop`, `iotop`, `sysstat`, `tcpdump`, `jq`, `strace`, `lsof`, `mtr`, `nmap`); installing packages during an outage wastes minutes and may be impossible on a full disk.
- Runbooks for the catalogue entries above, linked from alerts.
- Practise: break staging on purpose monthly and time the diagnosis (Part XI chapter 9; Part XIV project 6).

## Common mistakes

- Rebooting first, losing the evidence and often the cause.
- Fixing the symptom (freeing disk) without the cause (why it filled).
- Checking the application before the host.
- Not asking "what changed?"
- Working in silence; nobody else can help or learn.
- Declaring resolution without re-measuring the original symptom.

## Exercises

1. For each catalogue entry, write the one-command confirmation you would run first. Keep the list as a cheat sheet in your runbooks.
2. In a staging environment, cause three incidents from the catalogue (fill a disk, exhaust connections, expire a certificate on a test host) and time your diagnosis using the method.
3. Take your last real incident and rewrite its timeline using the method's steps; identify where time was lost.
