# AWS Accounts, Organizations and IAM

:::objectives
- Structure accounts with AWS Organizations so environments and clients are isolated, billed separately and governed centrally.
- Write and reason about IAM policies: identities, resources, actions, conditions, and the evaluation logic that combines them.
- Apply least privilege with roles, permission boundaries and service control policies.
- Audit IAM for the dangerous patterns.
:::

## The problem

Every AWS resource is protected by exactly one mechanism: IAM. A wrong policy is a public bucket, a deleted database, a stolen credential with admin rights. IAM is also where most teams accumulate the largest invisible debt: policies copied from Stack Overflow with `"Resource": "*"`, users with access keys from 2019, a single account where staging can delete production. Account structure and IAM discipline are the foundation everything in Part VII sits on.

## Accounts and Organizations

An account is the strongest isolation boundary AWS offers: separate IAM, separate billing line, separate service quotas, a blast radius wall. **AWS Organizations** groups accounts under a management account with consolidated billing, **Organizational Units** (OUs) for structure, and **Service Control Policies** (SCPs) that cap what any identity in a member account can do, root included.

**AWS Control Tower** automates this landing zone (accounts, OUs, baseline guardrails, centralised logging) and is the usual starting point; building the equivalent by hand with Organizations plus IaC is possible for teams who want control. When you deploy *into a client's own organisation* (Part XIII chapter 9), you are usually given an account in their Workloads OU and must live within their SCPs.

Baseline SCPs worth having: deny leaving the organisation; deny disabling CloudTrail, GuardDuty and Config; deny actions outside approved Regions (except global services); deny root user actions except a few; deny creating IAM users with access keys (force roles and SSO); deny deleting log archive buckets.

## IAM building blocks

- **Principals**: IAM users (avoid for humans; chapter 3), **roles** (assumed temporarily by services, federated users and other accounts), and AWS services.
- **Policies**: JSON documents granting (Allow) or denying (Deny) **actions** on **resources** under **conditions**. Identity-based policies attach to users/roles; **resource-based policies** attach to resources (S3 bucket policies, KMS key policies, SQS queue policies, role trust policies) and can grant access to principals in other accounts.
- **Managed policies** (AWS-managed like `AmazonS3ReadOnlyAccess`, or customer-managed) are reusable; **inline** policies are embedded in one identity. Prefer customer-managed policies in IaC; avoid AWS-managed policies broader than needed (`AdministratorAccess`, `PowerUserAccess` are not least privilege).
- **Permission boundaries**: a policy that caps the maximum permissions an identity can have, regardless of what is attached. Lets teams create roles for their workloads without being able to grant more than the boundary allows.
- **Trust policies**: on roles, defining *who may assume* the role (a service, an account, an OIDC provider, with conditions).
- **Session policies** and **STS**: temporary credentials with optional further restrictions (chapter 3).

## Policy evaluation

When a request arrives, IAM evaluates every applicable policy. The logic, simplified for the same-account case:

1. **Default deny**: nothing is allowed unless something allows it.
2. **Explicit Deny anywhere wins**: SCP, permission boundary, identity policy, resource policy or session policy with a matching Deny ends the evaluation.
3. **SCPs** must allow the action (they do not grant; they filter).
4. **Permission boundary** (if present) must allow.
5. **Identity policy or resource policy** must allow (within one account, either suffices; across accounts, both the identity's policy and the resource's policy must allow).
6. **Session policy** (if present) must allow.

Consequences: a resource policy can grant access without any identity policy within the account (how S3 bucket policies make buckets public even when no user has `s3:GetObject`); an SCP can block an administrator; a Deny statement is the only way to carve exceptions out of a broad Allow. The **IAM Policy Simulator** and **IAM Access Analyzer** (policy validation, unused access findings, external access findings) are the tools to test and audit.

## Roles everywhere

The pattern that makes IAM safe: **no long-lived credentials; identity flows through roles**.

- Workloads: instance profiles, ECS task roles, Lambda execution roles, EKS pod identities (Part IV chapter 18).
- Humans: SSO via Identity Center into permission sets (roles) with session durations (chapter 3).
- CI: OIDC federation from GitHub Actions/GitLab into a deploy role (Part X chapter 2).
- Cross-account: roles with trust policies naming the other account and an external ID; assume with STS (Part XIII chapter 9).
- Services acting on your behalf: service-linked roles and service roles (RDS enhanced monitoring, Config, Backup).

Audit: `aws iam list-users` should be empty or nearly so; `aws iam generate-credential-report` shows every user's key age and MFA status; Access Analyzer's unused-access findings list roles and permissions never exercised, which you remove.

## Conditions that matter

`aws:MultiFactorAuthPresent` (require MFA for sensitive actions), `aws:SourceIp`/`aws:SourceVpce` (restrict where calls may come from; careful with services that call on your behalf), `aws:PrincipalOrgID` (allow only principals from your organisation in resource policies: the standard guard against cross-account exposure), `aws:RequestedRegion` (in SCPs), `aws:ResourceTag`/`aws:PrincipalTag` (attribute-based access control: a role tagged `team=payments` may touch resources tagged `team=payments`), `s3:x-amz-server-side-encryption`, `kms:ViaService`, `iam:PassedToService` (which services a role may be passed to, a frequent privilege-escalation path when unrestricted), `ec2:ResourceTag` for instance-scoped operations.

## Privilege escalation paths

IAM has dozens of ways a limited identity can become an administrator if certain permissions are granted carelessly: `iam:PassRole` plus the ability to launch compute (attach an admin role to an instance you control), `iam:CreatePolicyVersion`, `iam:AttachUserPolicy`, `iam:UpdateAssumeRolePolicy` on a privileged role, `lambda:UpdateFunctionCode` on a function with a powerful role, `sts:AssumeRole` on `*`. Tools (Prowler, PMapper, Cloudsplaining) find these; the defence is scoping `iam:*` actions to specific resources, using permission boundaries for any role that creates roles, and never granting `PassRole` on `*`.

## Common mistakes

- One account for everything; staging credentials can destroy production.
- `AdministratorAccess` on application roles "temporarily".
- IAM users with access keys for humans and CI.
- `"Resource": "*"` on data-plane actions; `iam:PassRole` on `*`.
- Resource policies with `"Principal": "*"` and no condition.
- No SCPs; CloudTrail disabled by a compromised admin.
- Root user with access keys or without hardware MFA.
- Never reviewing unused permissions; roles accrete power forever.

## Exercises

1. In a sandbox organisation, create OUs and two accounts; attach an SCP denying actions outside one Region and denying CloudTrail changes; verify as an administrator in a member account that those actions fail.
2. Take an application role in a system you own and rewrite its policy to least privilege using Access Analyzer's generated policy from CloudTrail activity. Compare the before and after.
3. Run the IAM credential report and Access Analyzer on an account; eliminate every IAM user access key you can, and document the remaining ones with owners and rotation dates.
