## What the cloud provides

Cloud computing is renting computing resources (compute, storage, networking, databases, and hundreds of higher-level services) by the hour or request, provisioned by API in minutes, from a provider that operates the physical infrastructure at a scale no single client could. The economic shift is from capital expenditure (buy servers, amortise over years, over-provision for peaks) to operating expenditure (pay for what runs, scale with demand, release what you do not need). The engineering shift is that infrastructure becomes software: everything is an API call, therefore everything can be versioned, reviewed, tested and automated (Part X).

Service models: **IaaS** (virtual machines, networks, disks: EC2, VPC, EBS; you manage the OS and up), **PaaS/managed services** (RDS, ECS Fargate, App Runner; you manage the application and data), **serverless/FaaS** (Lambda, DynamoDB, S3; you manage code and configuration), **SaaS** (finished applications). Part V chapter 1's spectrum mapped onto AWS.

## Designing with Regions and AZs

- **Choose the Region** by: data residency and client policy (EU data in an EU Region), proximity to users (latency, Part II chapter 18), service availability (check that every service you need exists there), price (varies 10–30% between Regions), and the client's existing footprint. `us-east-1` is the oldest, cheapest and most feature-complete, and also the most frequently involved in large incidents; it is also where CloudFront certificates and some global service control planes live.
- **Use multiple AZs by default** for anything production: subnets in at least two (ideally three) AZs; ALB across them; application instances spread by the ASG or ECS; RDS Multi-AZ; ElastiCache with replicas in different AZs. The cost is modest (some cross-AZ data transfer charges, standby database instances) and it is the single biggest availability improvement available.
- **Multi-Region** is a different order of complexity and cost (Part IX chapter 10). For most clients: single Region, multi-AZ, with backups copied to a second Region for disaster recovery (Part V chapter 11).

What this means practically: AWS will never patch your EC2 instance's OS, close your open security group, rotate your IAM keys, enable your backups, or notice that your bucket is public (though services like Config, Security Hub and GuardDuty will *tell* you, if you turn them on). Every breach attributed to "the cloud" in the news was a customer-side misconfiguration or credential problem; the shared responsibility model is why Part IV chapter 24 reads the way it does.

For compliance conversations with clients: AWS publishes its attestations (SOC 1/2/3, ISO 27001, PCI DSS, HIPAA eligibility) through AWS Artifact; these cover AWS's side. Your side (configuration, access, data handling) is what auditors examine in your workload, and the **AWS Well-Architected Framework** (its Security, Reliability, Cost Optimization, Performance, Operational Excellence and Sustainability pillars) is the vocabulary clients' architects will use. Run a Well-Architected review on significant systems; it is free and produces a defensible document.

## Accounts and the console

An **AWS account** is the top-level container for resources and billing, identified by a 12-digit ID. The **root user** (email and password) owns the account and should be used only for the handful of tasks that require it, with hardware MFA, no access keys, and credentials locked away. Everything else happens through IAM identities (chapter 2) and, for humans, through IAM Identity Center SSO (chapter 3). Multiple accounts under AWS Organizations separate environments and clients (chapter 2).

The console is for exploring and emergencies; the CLI and SDKs are for work; infrastructure as code is for anything that persists (Part X). Console-created resources are undocumented and unreproducible; treat them as technical debt from the moment they exist.

## Cost model basics

You pay for: compute time (per second or hour), storage (per GB-month), requests (per million), data transfer **out** to the Internet and between Regions/AZs (in is free; out is where bills surprise), and hourly charges for managed components (load balancers, NAT gateways, cluster control planes). Chapter 4 covers monitoring and the usual surprises. The free tier (12 months of small instances and permanent allowances on some services) is useful for learning and dangerous for assumptions: a "free" t2.micro behind a NAT gateway costs $35/month.

## Common misconceptions

- "AWS keeps my data safe." AWS keeps its infrastructure safe; your data's safety depends on your IAM, encryption, backup and access choices.
- "It is in the cloud, so it is highly available." A single instance in a single AZ is as fragile as a single server anywhere.
- "Regions are interchangeable." Services, prices and compliance differ; data residency is a legal matter.
- "The free tier means free." Many adjacent charges are not in the free tier.
