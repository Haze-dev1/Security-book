# Root Cause Analysis and Blameless Postmortems

:::objectives
- Run a postmortem that finds contributing factors rather than a culprit, and produces changes that actually prevent recurrence.
- Apply structured analysis techniques (timeline reconstruction, five whys with care, contributing-factor analysis) and avoid their failure modes.
- Write a postmortem document people will read, and track action items to completion.
:::

:::prereq
[On-Call and Incident Response](#/p11/c06).
:::

## Why blameless

Incidents in complex systems rarely have a single root cause; they emerge from several ordinary conditions combining: a configuration change that looked harmless, an alert routed to the wrong channel, a dependency that behaved differently under load, a runbook that was out of date. The person who typed the command is the last link in that chain, not its cause. If postmortems punish that person, people hide mistakes, information stops flowing, and the organisation loses the only thing an incident offers: the chance to learn how the system really behaves. **Blameless** does not mean consequence-free for negligence or malice; it means the analysis assumes people acted reasonably given what they knew, and asks what made the reasonable action harmful.

## When to hold one

Any SEV1 or SEV2; any incident that consumed a meaningful share of error budget; any data loss or security event; any near miss that scared someone; any incident where the response itself went badly even if impact was small. Within 48 hours while memory is fresh; with the responders, the owners of involved systems, and a facilitator who was not deeply involved.

## Reconstructing the timeline

The timeline is the evidence base. Build it from the incident channel, alert timestamps, deployment records, CloudTrail, dashboards and chat, in UTC, with sources:

```text
08:51  Deploy of portal-api sha-3f2a9c1 to production started (deployment record)
08:58  Deployment complete; canary analysis passed (CodeDeploy)
09:04  RDS connections rise from 40 to 180 (CloudWatch)
09:07  First 5xx responses; error ratio 0.4% (Prometheus)
09:12  PortalHighErrorRate fires (Alertmanager) → routed to #portal-alerts (Slack), NOT to PagerDuty [routing rule matched env=prod, label was env=production]
09:31  Customer support reports three tickets about login failures
09:34  Support escalates to on-call via phone; incident declared SEV2
09:36  IC assigned; dashboard shows connections at pool limit; error ratio 4%
09:41  Decision: roll back to sha-9d1e0b7 (previous digest from deployment record)
09:46  Rollback complete; connections fall to 45; error ratio to 0.1%
09:52  Probes green; incident mitigated; monitoring
10:20  Resolved. Impact: 45 minutes, ~3.8% of requests failed, ~2,100 users affected (support + logs estimate)
```

Note what the timeline already reveals: detection took 27 minutes longer than it should have because of a label mismatch in alert routing. That is a contributing factor nobody would have called "the root cause".

## Analysis techniques

**Five whys**, used carefully: ask why repeatedly to move from symptom to systemic condition. Danger: it produces a single linear chain and stops where the facilitator is satisfied. Use it as one thread among several, and branch when there are multiple answers.

```text
Why did requests fail?            → The connection pool was exhausted.
Why was the pool exhausted?       → The new version opened a connection per request in the new reporting endpoint.
Why did that pass review?         → The ORM helper used looks like the pooled one; the difference is not documented.
Why did canary analysis pass?     → The canary received 10% of traffic for 5 minutes; the reporting endpoint is hit by a 09:00 scheduled job, after the canary window.
Why was detection slow?           → Alert label mismatch routed the page to Slack; nobody watches that channel in the morning.
```

**Contributing factors analysis**: list every condition that, had it been different, would have prevented or reduced the incident, in categories: change (what was deployed), detection (how we found out), response (what slowed mitigation), design (what made the system fragile), process (reviews, tests, runbooks), external (dependencies). This produces a richer set of fixes than any single root cause.

**Counterfactuals to avoid**: "the engineer should have been more careful" is not a factor; "the API makes the unpooled helper the obvious choice" is. Rephrase every human-error finding as a system condition.

**What went well**: rollback was one command and took five minutes because the previous digest was in the deployment record; the IC role worked; support escalation worked. Protect these.

## Action items that stick

Good action items are specific, owned, dated, tracked in the normal backlog with priority, and target the contributing factors, not the person:

| Finding | Weak action | Strong action |
|---|---|---|
| Unpooled DB helper misused | "Be careful with DB helpers" | Remove the unpooled helper or rename it `unsafe_direct_connection`; add a lint rule; document pooling in the data access guide (owner: A, due: 2 weeks) |
| Canary did not exercise the endpoint | "Longer canaries" | Canary analysis includes per-route error rate and must cover a scheduled-job window or trigger the job synthetically (owner: B, 3 weeks) |
| Alert misrouted | "Fix the label" | Fix the label; add a CI test that every `severity=page` rule in production matches a PagerDuty route; add a weekly synthetic page test (owner: C, 1 week) |
| Connection pool limit silent | "Monitor connections" | Alert at 80% of pool capacity with runbook; dashboard panel (owner: A, 1 week) |

Track completion in postmortem reviews; an organisation whose action items are 60% open after three months is not learning. Prioritise fixes to detection and mitigation highly: they help with every future incident, not just this one.

## The document

@file: postmortems/2026-11-01-portal-login-errors.md (template)
```markdown
# Portal login errors — 2026-11-01

**Severity**: SEV2 · **Duration**: 09:04–09:52 UTC (48 min impact) · **Owner**: portal team · **Status**: action items in progress

## Summary (3–4 sentences for executives)
A deploy introduced an unpooled database connection path in a new reporting endpoint. A scheduled 09:00 job exhausted the connection pool, causing ~3.8% of requests to fail for 45 minutes, affecting about 2,100 users attempting to sign in. Detection was delayed 27 minutes by an alert routing misconfiguration. Rollback restored service in five minutes once the incident was declared.

## Impact
- Users: ~2,100 failed sign-ins; 14 support tickets; no data loss.
- SLO: consumed 11% of the monthly availability error budget.

## Timeline (UTC)
(as above)

## Contributing factors
1. Change: new endpoint used an unpooled DB helper that resembles the pooled one.
2. Testing: canary window did not include the scheduled job that drives this endpoint's load.
3. Detection: alert label `env=production` did not match routing matcher `env=prod`; page became a Slack message.
4. Design: no alert on pool saturation; pool limit lower than task count × per-task maximum.

## What went well
- Rollback was one command using the previous digest from the deployment record.
- Support escalation path worked; IC and scribe roles were followed.

## Action items
| # | Action | Owner | Due | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Remove/rename unpooled helper; lint rule | A | 2026-11-15 | open |
| 2 | Canary covers scheduled-job window | B | 2026-11-22 | open |
| 3 | CI test for alert routing; weekly synthetic page | C | 2026-11-08 | done |
| 4 | Pool saturation alert and runbook | A | 2026-11-08 | done |

## Lessons
Resemblance between safe and unsafe APIs is a design defect. Alert routing is production code and needs tests. Canaries must see representative load, not just representative time.
```

Write for a reader who was not there; avoid names in the narrative ("an engineer deployed" rather than "Sam deployed"); publish internally where every team can read it. Many organisations publish customer-facing versions for major incidents; honesty builds more trust than silence.

## Facilitating the meeting

Sixty minutes, agenda: walk the timeline together and correct it (20 min); contributing factors brainstorm, everyone contributes, no interruptions (20 min); what went well (5 min); action items with owners and dates (15 min). The facilitator enforces blamelessness by rephrasing ("so the system allowed X to happen because...") and keeps the group from stopping at the first satisfying cause. Decisions about priority of action items can go to the owning team's planning, but the items themselves leave the room written down.

## Patterns across postmortems

Review postmortems quarterly as a set: recurring factors (detection delays, deploy-related incidents, a fragile dependency) point to systemic investments: better canaries, a dependency replacement, alert testing. Track metrics: time to detect, time to mitigate, incidents per deploy, action item completion rate.

## Common mistakes

- Stopping at "human error".
- One root cause, one action item, usually "add a check".
- Postmortems as documents nobody reads; hold the meeting, share the summary widely.
- Action items without owners or dates, or filed and forgotten.
- Skipping near misses: they are free lessons.
- Blame disguised as process ("we will add an approval step") that slows everyone and fixes nothing.

## Exercises

1. Take a real or simulated incident and write the full postmortem using the template, with at least four contributing factors across different categories and strong action items.
2. Facilitate a postmortem meeting for a colleague's incident; practise rephrasing blame statements into system conditions.
3. Review the last five postmortems in your organisation (or published ones from major companies) and identify recurring factors and the systemic investment they suggest.
