# Monitoring versus Observability: SLIs, SLOs, SLAs and Error Budgets

:::objectives
- Define service level indicators, objectives and agreements precisely and choose SLIs that reflect user experience.
- Compute error budgets and use them to make reliability versus velocity decisions explicitly.
- Build burn-rate alerts that page on real risk to the objective rather than on noise.
:::

## Monitoring versus observability

**Monitoring** watches known failure modes with predefined checks: CPU over 80%, disk full, process down, error rate above a threshold. It answers "is something we anticipated wrong?" **Observability** is the ability to ask arbitrary new questions of rich telemetry when something unanticipated happens: "why are only tenants in the EU on version 3.4 seeing slow exports since 09:12?" Monitoring is necessary; observability is what gets you through the incidents monitoring did not predict. Both are built on the signals in chapters 1–3; the difference is in how you use them and how much context (high-cardinality attributes in traces and logs) you keep.

## SLI, SLO, SLA

- **SLI (indicator)**: a measurement of one aspect of service quality, expressed as a ratio of good events to total events over a window. "The proportion of HTTP requests to the API that return non-5xx within 500 ms."
- **SLO (objective)**: a target for an SLI over a period. "99.9% of requests good over a rolling 30 days." Internal, chosen by the team and the business, usually stricter than any contract.
- **SLA (agreement)**: a contract with customers with consequences (credits, penalties) for missing a target, usually looser than the SLO and with carefully defined exclusions and measurement methods. "99.5% monthly uptime measured by our monitoring, excluding scheduled maintenance; 10% credit below that."

The gap between SLO and SLA is the safety margin: breach the SLO and you take internal action; breach the SLA and you pay.

## Choosing SLIs that matter

Measure what users experience, as close to them as possible, using a small number of indicators per user journey:

| Journey type | SLI | Measured at |
|---|---|---|
| Request/response | Availability: non-5xx responses / all valid requests. Latency: requests faster than threshold / all | Load balancer or edge (sees failures the application cannot report), plus synthetic probes |
| Data pipeline / async | Freshness: records processed within N minutes / all. Correctness: records passing validation / all | Pipeline output |
| Storage | Durability: objects retrievable / objects stored. Availability of reads and writes | Client-side probes |
| Batch job | Completion within window / scheduled runs | Scheduler |
| Client app | Successful screen loads / attempts; crash-free sessions | RUM / mobile SDK |

Exclude requests that are the client's fault (4xx) from the denominator unless they indicate your bug (a spike in 401s after a deploy). Measure latency at a percentile that reflects the objective: "99% of requests under 500 ms" is a statement about p99. Choose thresholds from user research or business reality, not from current performance.

## Error budgets

If the SLO is 99.9% over 30 days, the **error budget** is 0.1% of events (or of time) that may be bad without breaching: for 100 million requests per month, 100,000 bad requests; in time terms, 43.2 minutes of full outage. The budget is the amount of unreliability you are *allowed* to spend, on deploys, experiments, risky migrations, or incidents.

The error budget makes reliability decisions explicit and shared:

- **Budget healthy**: ship fast, run experiments, take the migration risk.
- **Budget mostly spent**: slow down; prioritise reliability work; require more review for risky changes.
- **Budget exhausted**: feature freeze except for reliability fixes until the budget recovers; the policy is agreed in advance with product leadership so it is not negotiated during the crisis.

An **error budget policy** document records: the SLOs, the measurement method, what happens at each threshold (50%, 75%, 100% consumed), who decides exceptions, and how the policy is reviewed. The budget turns "is the system reliable enough?" from an argument into a number both engineering and product accept.

## Burn-rate alerts

Alerting on the SLO directly ("we are below 99.9% this month") fires too late. Alerting on instantaneous error rate fires too often. **Burn rate** is how fast you are consuming the budget relative to the sustainable rate: burn rate 1 means you will spend exactly the budget over the window; burn rate 14.4 means you will spend the whole 30-day budget in 2 days.

Multi-window, multi-burn-rate alerting (from the Google SRE workbook) pages on fast burns and tickets on slow ones, with a short window to confirm the problem is still happening:

| Severity | Burn rate | Long window | Short window | Budget consumed if sustained |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Page | 14.4 | 1 h | 5 m | 2% in 1 hour |
| Page | 6 | 6 h | 30 m | 5% in 6 hours |
| Ticket | 3 | 1 d | 2 h | 10% in 1 day |
| Ticket | 1 | 3 d | 6 h | 10% in 3 days |

```yaml
# Prometheus: fast-burn page for a 99.9% availability SLO
- alert: PortalAvailabilityFastBurn
  expr: |
    (
      sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="portal-api",status_class="5xx"}[1h]))
        / sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="portal-api"}[1h]))
    ) > (14.4 * 0.001)
    and
    (
      sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="portal-api",status_class="5xx"}[5m]))
        / sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="portal-api"}[5m]))
    ) > (14.4 * 0.001)
  for: 2m
  labels: { severity: page, slo: portal-availability }
  annotations:
    summary: "portal-api burning 30d error budget at 14.4x (2% per hour)"
    runbook_url: https://runbooks.acme.example/portal/slo-burn
```

The long window makes the alert significant (enough bad events to matter); the short window makes it current (the problem is still happening, so a page is actionable and resets quickly when fixed). Tools like Sloth or Pyrra generate these rules from an SLO definition.

## Writing an SLO document

```checklist
# SLO definition for one user journey
- Journey and users: who does what, and why it matters
- SLI specification: numerator, denominator, exclusions, measurement point
- SLI implementation: the exact query or pipeline that computes it
- Objective and window: e.g. 99.9% over rolling 30 days
- Error budget policy: actions at 50/75/100% consumption; decision owners
- Alerting: burn-rate thresholds and routing
- Dashboard: where the SLI and budget are visible
- Review cadence: SLO revisited quarterly against user feedback and cost
```

A system's availability is bounded by its serial dependencies: a service at 99.95% that hard-depends on a database at 99.95% and an identity provider at 99.9% cannot exceed roughly 99.8% unless it degrades gracefully when they fail. Measure dependencies' real behaviour, not their marketing numbers.

## SLAs in client work

:::fde Writing or accepting an SLA
Before promising an SLA to a client: define the SLI measurement method and source (your monitoring, not theirs); exclude scheduled maintenance with notice periods, client-caused incidents, force majeure and third-party outages beyond your control (with care, since clients buy outcomes); set the SLA below your SLO; define the remedy and its cap; state how disputes are measured. Then confirm the architecture can actually deliver the SLO you set internally, with margin, and that the support model (on-call hours!) matches the promise. An SLA with 24×7 99.9% uptime and business-hours support is a contradiction a client will eventually notice.
:::

## Common mistakes

- SLIs measured where the application cannot see failures (inside the app rather than at the edge).
- Too many SLOs; nobody tracks them. Three to five per service, tied to user journeys.
- SLOs set at current performance ("we are at 99.97% so the SLO is 99.97%"), leaving no budget to spend.
- Alerting on SLO breach rather than on burn rate.
- Error budget policies agreed only among engineers; product leadership first hears about the freeze when it happens.
- Treating 100% as a goal: the last nine costs more than it is worth for almost every service.

## Exercises

1. Define SLIs and SLOs for three user journeys of a service you know; implement the SLI queries at the edge and build the SLO dashboard with budget remaining.
2. Generate multi-window burn-rate alerts (by hand or with Sloth) and verify the fast-burn alert fires within minutes under an injected 5% error rate and resolves when fixed.
3. Draft an error budget policy and an SLA clause for a client; check the SLA is achievable given dependency availabilities and support hours.
