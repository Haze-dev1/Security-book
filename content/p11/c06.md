# On-Call and Incident Response

:::objectives
- Run a humane, effective on-call rotation: schedules, escalation, handoffs, compensation and limits.
- Lead an incident from detection to resolution using defined roles, a structured process and clear communication.
- Communicate with stakeholders and customers during incidents honestly and usefully.
:::

:::prereq
[Health Checks and Alerting Strategies](#/p11/c05).
:::

## On-call that people can sustain

On-call means being reachable and able to respond within a defined time when paged. Done badly it burns people out and drives attrition; done well it is a normal, bounded responsibility.

- **Rotation**: at least four to six people per rotation so each person is on call no more than one week in four; primary and secondary; follow-the-sun across time zones where possible.
- **Response expectation**: acknowledge within 5–15 minutes, begin working within 30; beyond that escalate automatically to the secondary, then to the engineering manager.
- **Load limits**: more than two pages per night or a handful per week means the system, not the person, needs fixing; track and act on it. Time off after a bad night is policy, not a favour.
- **Compensation**: paid on-call allowances or time in lieu; recognise that carrying a pager is work.
- **Readiness**: laptop, VPN, credentials, access to runbooks and dashboards tested *before* the shift; a pre-shift checklist.
- **Handoff**: a written summary at each rotation boundary: open incidents, ongoing risks, recent changes, silences in place.
- **Authority**: on-call engineers may roll back, scale, enable degradation modes and page anyone without asking permission; the policy says so in writing.

:::fde On-call for client systems
Define it in the contract: hours of coverage, response times by severity, who at the client can declare an incident, how they reach you, and what you may do in their account without approval. A client who expects 24×7 response for a system contracted with business-hours support will discover the gap during an outage.
:::

## Incident severity

| Severity | Definition | Response | Communication |
|---|---|---|---|
| SEV1 | Critical functionality down for most users, data loss or security breach in progress | All hands; incident commander; page leadership | Status page within 15 min; updates every 30 min; executive briefing |
| SEV2 | Major degradation or a key feature down for many users; workaround may exist | On-call plus relevant owners; incident commander | Status page; updates hourly |
| SEV3 | Minor feature impaired or small user subset affected | On-call during business hours | Internal channel; customers informed if asked |
| SEV4 | Cosmetic or internal-only issue | Ticket | None |

Declare severity early based on observed impact, and adjust. Over-declaring and downgrading is cheaper than under-declaring and scrambling.

## Roles

In anything above a trivial incident, separate the roles so one person is not debugging, coordinating and communicating at once:

- **Incident Commander (IC)**: owns the incident; decides priorities and actions; keeps the team focused; does not debug. Anyone trained can be IC; seniority is irrelevant.
- **Operations / technical lead**: directs the hands-on investigation and mitigation; reports to the IC.
- **Communications lead**: writes status updates, handles stakeholders and support, shields the technical team from interruptions.
- **Scribe**: keeps the timeline (timestamps, actions, observations, decisions) in the incident channel or document; the postmortem depends on it.
- **Subject matter experts**: paged in as needed; leave when done.

## Running the response

The IC's loop every 10–15 minutes: *What do we know? What are we doing? Who is doing it? When do we check back?* Assign each action to a named person with a time; avoid five people independently restarting things. Keep the channel for the incident only; move speculation elsewhere. Use the runbooks; when they do not fit, record what you tried.

Decision principles under pressure:

- **Mitigate first.** Rollbacks, failovers and feature kill switches are reversible; investigations can wait.
- **Change one thing at a time** and observe, unless impact is total and you are stacking known-safe mitigations.
- **Protect data** over availability when they conflict: stopping writes to a corrupting system is better than staying up.
- **Preserve evidence** before destructive fixes (snapshot the disk before rebuilding; copy logs before rotating).
- **Escalate early**: paging a specialist who turns out to be unnecessary costs minutes; not paging them costs hours.
- **Watch for the second incident**: responders tired and changing production quickly cause follow-on failures; the IC guards against hasty, untested changes.
- **Security incidents** follow the same structure plus containment, legal and regulatory notification steps ([Incident Response Guide](#/p15/c04)).

## Communication

Internal updates (channel, bridge) every 15–30 minutes even if nothing changed ("still investigating; next update at 10:30"). Status page updates for customer-visible incidents within 15 minutes of declaration, written for customers: what is affected, what is not, what you are doing, when the next update comes. No speculation about causes, no blame, no jargon, no "a small number of users" if you do not know. Support gets a one-paragraph script. Leadership gets impact, ETA if known, and what you need from them.

```text
Investigating — 09:42 UTC
We are investigating elevated error rates affecting sign-in and document downloads for some customers. Other features are operating normally. Next update by 10:15 UTC.

Identified — 10:05 UTC
We have identified the cause as a recent configuration change and are rolling it back. Sign-in is recovering; downloads remain affected. Next update by 10:30 UTC.

Resolved — 10:24 UTC
Service is fully restored as of 10:18 UTC. The issue lasted approximately 48 minutes. We will publish a postmortem within five business days.
```

## Tooling

Incident management platforms (PagerDuty, Opsgenie, incident.io, FireHydrant, Rootly) automate: declaration (`/incident declare`), channel and bridge creation, role assignment, timeline capture from chat, status page updates, reminders for updates, and postmortem templates. Without them, a template channel name, a shared document for the timeline, and a status page product cover the essentials. The point is that under stress nobody should have to remember the process.

## After the incident

Close the loop: final update, evidence preserved, timeline cleaned, postmortem scheduled with the right people, action items tracked to completion (chapter 7). Review the on-call experience: was the alert useful, was the runbook right, was escalation smooth? Feed every answer back into alerts and runbooks.

## Common mistakes

- Debugging root cause while users suffer, when a rollback was available.
- No IC: everyone investigates, nobody coordinates, three people make conflicting changes.
- Silence toward stakeholders because "we don't know yet"; say that, with a time for the next update.
- Declaring incidents only for total outages; degradation incidents never get analysed.
- Heroics as culture: the same two people handle every incident, until they leave.
- Skipping the postmortem because the fix was "obvious".

## Exercises

1. Design an on-call rotation and escalation policy for a team of six, including response expectations, handoff template and load limits; configure it in a tool or a calendar with escalation rules.
2. Run a tabletop incident: give a group a scenario (database failover failed during a deploy) and have them practise the roles, timeline and status updates for 45 minutes.
3. Write status page templates for investigating, identified, monitoring and resolved states for your service.
