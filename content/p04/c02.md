# Threat Modeling and Trust Boundaries

:::objectives
- Run a lightweight, repeatable threat modeling session for a feature or system in under two hours.
- Draw a data-flow diagram with trust boundaries and use STRIDE to enumerate threats at each one.
- Rate and prioritise threats, choose mitigations, and record them so they are tested and maintained.
- Integrate threat modeling into design reviews and client discovery.
:::

:::prereq
Chapter 1.
:::

## The problem

Security review at the end of a project finds the problems when they are most expensive to fix. Threat modeling moves the question "how could this be attacked?" to the design stage, where the answer changes the architecture rather than patching it. It is also the single most useful artefact for a client conversation about security, because it shows *what* you considered and *why* you chose each control, rather than a list of tools.

The method here is deliberately lightweight. A two-hour session with a whiteboard beats a forty-page document nobody reads.

## The four questions

Adam Shostack's framing, used by most practical teams:

1. **What are we working on?** Draw it.
2. **What can go wrong?** Enumerate threats systematically.
3. **What are we going to do about it?** Mitigate, accept, transfer or eliminate.
4. **Did we do a good job?** Verify mitigations exist and work; revisit when the system changes.

## Step 1: data-flow diagram with trust boundaries

Draw the system as processes (your services), data stores, external entities (users, third parties) and the data flows between them. Then draw **trust boundaries**: lines where the level of trust changes. Across every boundary, data must be validated and the sender authenticated as appropriate.

## Step 2: enumerate threats with STRIDE

For each element and especially each boundary-crossing flow, ask the six STRIDE questions:

| Threat | Violates | Question | Example in the export feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| **S**poofing | Authentication | Can someone pretend to be another user, service or system? | A forged job message with another tenant's ID; a phished support account |
| **T**ampering | Integrity | Can data be modified in transit or at rest without detection? | Export CSV altered in S3; job payload modified in the queue |
| **R**epudiation | Non-repudiation | Can an actor deny having done something? | Support staff exports a customer's data and no record exists |
| **I**nformation disclosure | Confidentiality | Can data be read by someone not authorised? | Export includes other tenants' rows; presigned URL forwarded; link in email logs; CSV injection reveals data when opened |
| **D**enial of service | Availability | Can someone exhaust resources or block legitimate use? | A user requests 1,000 exports; a giant tenant export locks the database |
| **E**levation of privilege | Authorization | Can someone do more than they are allowed? | A regular user triggers a support-only export via the API; worker role can read all buckets |

Work through the flows methodically. Most threats will be familiar from the vulnerability chapters in this part; the value is in systematically *asking*, not in cleverness.

## Step 3: rate and decide

Rate each threat by **likelihood** (how easy, how motivated an attacker, how exposed) and **impact** (CIA damage, number of users, regulatory consequence). A simple High/Medium/Low grid is enough; numerical scoring (CVSS, DREAD) adds false precision without more insight for most teams.

For each threat choose one of four:

- **Mitigate**: add a control. "Worker derives tenant from the job's signed payload and sets RLS context; exports bucket has per-tenant prefixes; presigned URLs expire in 15 minutes; CSV fields are escaped against formula injection; per-user export rate limit of 5/hour; every export logged with requester, target, time."
- **Eliminate**: remove the feature or the data. "Do not include payment card fields in exports at all."
- **Transfer**: move the risk to a party better placed to handle it. "Use the email provider's link-tracking opt-out; rely on S3's encryption and durability."
- **Accept**: consciously, with a named owner and a review date. "A customer who forwards their own export link within 15 minutes exposes their own data; accepted."

Record every decision. The table of threats, ratings and decisions *is* the threat model.

## Step 4: verify and maintain

Each mitigation becomes a requirement with a test or a check: an integration test that a user cannot export another tenant; a configuration check that the bucket is private; an alert on export volume anomalies. Link them from the model. Revisit the model when the design changes (new data flow, new integration, new role), when an incident reveals a missed threat, and at least annually.

## Trust boundaries in common architectures

- **Browser ↔ API**: everything crossing is untrusted; authenticate, validate, authorise, rate limit.
- **API ↔ internal services**: often treated as trusted "because it is inside the VPC". It should not be: authenticate service-to-service (IAM, mTLS, signed tokens), validate inputs, and assume one compromised service tries to reach others. This is the [[zero trust]] posture.
- **Application ↔ queue ↔ worker**: the queue is a boundary; a message is input. Sign or at least validate payloads; derive authority from the message's recorded requester, not from a field anyone can set.
- **Your system ↔ third parties**: their webhooks are untrusted input (verify signatures); your calls to them carry your credentials (least privilege API keys, scoped per integration).
- **Build pipeline ↔ production**: a boundary attackers love. Pipeline credentials, dependency sources and build scripts are all inputs to production.
- **Operators ↔ production**: humans with access are a boundary; MFA, least privilege, audit.

## Threat modeling with clients

:::fde
In discovery (Part XIII chapter 2), the data-flow diagram is the fastest way to understand a client's system and the most persuasive way to explain a risk. Draw it with them. Mark the boundaries. Ask the STRIDE questions out loud. Clients who would resist "a security audit" readily engage with "let's draw how data moves and see where it could go wrong." The resulting one-pager is a deliverable they can show their own stakeholders, and it anchors every later security decision in agreed reasoning.
:::

## Common mistakes

- Threat modeling once, at the start, never again.
- Modeling the whole platform at once and producing nothing actionable.
- Listing threats without decisions, or decisions without tests.
- Treating the VPC as a trust zone where nothing needs authentication.
- Rating with elaborate numeric schemes that consume the session.
- Writing a document instead of a table on a whiteboard.
- Forgetting non-code assets: the build pipeline, operator access, backups, logs.

## Exercises

1. Threat model one feature you are building now: diagram, STRIDE table, decisions. Time-box to 90 minutes.
2. For each mitigation in your table, identify the test or check that proves it exists. Write at least two of the missing tests.
3. Take a client's or your company's existing architecture diagram and add trust boundaries. Identify one flow currently treated as trusted that should not be.
