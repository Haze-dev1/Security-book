# Data Privacy and Personally Identifiable Information

:::objectives
- Classify data by sensitivity and apply proportionate controls to each class.
- Apply privacy principles (minimisation, purpose limitation, retention, transparency) in system design.
- Implement the mechanics regulations require: data inventories, deletion and export, consent records, breach notification readiness, processor agreements.
- Handle personal data responsibly in logs, analytics, backups, non-production environments and third parties.
:::

:::prereq
Chapters 1, 2, 16 and 22.
:::

## The problem

Every client system holds personal data: customers, employees, patients, end users. Laws (GDPR in the EU and UK, CCPA/CPRA in California, LGPD in Brazil, HIPAA for US health data, PCI DSS for card data by contract, and many others) impose obligations on how it is collected, used, protected, retained and deleted, with fines and mandatory breach disclosure. Beyond the law, a client's customers trust them with data, and a Forward Deployed Engineer who copies the production database to a laptop "to debug" has broken that trust regardless of outcome. Privacy is an engineering property: you design systems that *can* comply, or you build an incident.

This chapter is engineering guidance, not legal advice; the client's legal or data protection function decides obligations, and you implement them.

## Vocabulary

- **Personal data / [[pii]]**: anything relating to an identifiable person: names, emails, IDs, IP addresses, device identifiers, location, behavioural data, and combinations that identify someone.
- **Special categories / sensitive data**: health, biometrics, ethnicity, religion, sexual orientation, political opinions, criminal data; children's data. Higher protection, often explicit consent.
- **Controller**: decides why and how data is processed (usually the client). **Processor**: processes on the controller's behalf (often you, and your cloud providers). Processors need a **data processing agreement** (DPA) and must follow instructions.
- **Data subject**: the person. Has **rights**: access (a copy of their data), rectification, erasure ("right to be forgotten"), portability, restriction, objection.
- **Lawful basis**: consent, contract, legal obligation, legitimate interest, etc. Determines what you may do.
- **Breach**: unauthorised access, loss or disclosure; often notifiable to regulators within 72 hours (GDPR) and to individuals when risk is high.

## Classification

| Class | Examples | Controls |
|---|---|---|
| **Public** | Marketing content, public docs | Integrity only |
| **Internal** | Architecture docs, non-sensitive config | Access control |
| **Confidential** | Business data, customer lists, contracts | Encryption at rest, least-privilege access, audit |
| **Personal (PII)** | Names, emails, addresses, IPs, behaviour | All of the above, plus minimisation, retention limits, subject rights support, DPAs |
| **Sensitive personal / regulated** | Health, financial account data, government IDs, card data, children's data | Field-level encryption, strict need-to-know, enhanced audit (reads), tokenisation, regulatory scope (HIPAA, PCI) |
| **Secrets** | Credentials, keys | Chapter 17 |

Tag data at the schema level (a column comment or a data catalogue entry: `pii`, `sensitive`) so that tooling, reviews and retention jobs can find it. A **data inventory** (what personal data, where it lives, why, for how long, who accesses it, which processors) is both a legal requirement (records of processing) and the map you need for every control below.

## Design principles

**Minimisation**: collect only what the purpose needs. Do you need date of birth, or just "over 18"? Full address, or country for tax? Every field you do not collect cannot leak.

**Purpose limitation**: data collected for order fulfilment is not automatically available for marketing or model training. Enforce in design: separate stores or explicit flags, and access paths tied to purposes.

**Retention**: define a retention period per data category (and per legal requirement) and **implement deletion**: scheduled jobs that delete or anonymise expired records, including in backups' lifecycle, logs, analytics and search indexes. "We keep everything forever" is a liability, not a feature.

**Pseudonymisation and anonymisation**: replace identifiers with tokens (pseudonymisation: reversible with a key kept elsewhere; reduces exposure and enables analytics on less sensitive data) or remove identifiability irreversibly (anonymisation: aggregate, generalise, add noise; true anonymisation is hard, and re-identification from "anonymous" datasets is a well-documented risk).

**Transparency**: the privacy notice must match what the system does. Engineers often add a data flow (a new analytics tool, a new third-party API) that nobody told legal about; make "new processor or new purpose" a design-review checkbox.

**Security proportionate to risk**: the controls of this whole part, applied with intensity matching the classification.

## Implementing subject rights

- **Access and portability**: an export per user of all their personal data across systems, in a common format (JSON/CSV), delivered securely (authenticated download, short-lived link), within the legal window (one month under GDPR). Build it as a job over the data inventory, not an ad-hoc SQL session.
- **Erasure**: delete or anonymise the subject's data across the primary database, caches, search indexes, object storage, analytics, logs (within retention), and notify processors to do the same. Keep what law requires (invoices for tax) with a documented basis, and pseudonymise instead where integrity matters (audit logs, chapter 22). Backups: most regulators accept deletion from backups on their normal rotation if restored backups are re-purged; document the approach.
- **Rectification**: edit flows that propagate to derived data.
- **Consent management**: record what was consented to, when, how, and the version of the notice; honour withdrawal immediately across systems (marketing flags, cookie consent for non-essential cookies and trackers).

Design the identity graph first: all personal data must be findable by subject ID. Data keyed only by email in a third-party tool with no link to your user IDs will be missed.

## Personal data in the places people forget

- **Logs**: request bodies, query strings with emails, IPs, user agents, stack traces containing record contents. Redact at the logging layer (allow-list fields; mask patterns for emails, card numbers, tokens); set retention; treat log stores as personal-data stores.
- **Analytics and error tracking**: Sentry events with user context, product analytics with free-text fields, session replay tools recording form input. Configure scrubbing; disable replay on sensitive pages; sign DPAs.
- **Non-production environments**: never raw production PII (Part I chapter 11); use synthetic data or verified masking.
- **Backups and exports**: encrypted, access-controlled, retained per policy, included in deletion procedures.
- **Caches and queues**: PII in Redis and SQS messages inherits none of the database's controls; minimise what is placed there and set TTLs.
- **Emails and notifications**: content stored by the email provider; links containing IDs.
- **Third parties and AI tools**: sending customer data to an LLM API or a SaaS tool is processing by a processor; it needs a DPA, a purpose, and often explicit client approval. Many clients forbid it without review.
- **Developer laptops and chat**: CSV exports, screenshots, log snippets pasted into Slack or tickets. Policy, training and tooling (redaction, short-lived access) all matter.
- **Cross-border transfers**: storing EU residents' data in a US region, or support staff in another country accessing it, triggers transfer rules. Region choice is a privacy decision; make it with the client's legal function.

## Breach readiness

You will handle a suspected breach eventually. Prepare: an incident process that includes legal and the client's DPO from the first hour (Part XI chapter 6); audit logs that let you scope what was accessed (chapter 22); a data inventory to determine which subjects and categories are involved; templates for regulator and subject notifications; a decision log. The 72-hour clock starts at awareness; the quality of your logs determines whether you can say "17 records of category X" or must say "possibly everything".

## Regulations in one paragraph each

**GDPR / UK GDPR**: applies to personal data of people in the EU/UK regardless of where you are; lawful basis, rights, DPAs, records of processing, DPIAs for high-risk processing, breach notification in 72 hours, fines up to 4% of global turnover. **CCPA/CPRA**: California residents; disclosure, opt-out of sale/sharing, deletion, non-discrimination. **HIPAA**: US protected health information; covered entities and business associates (you sign a BAA), administrative/physical/technical safeguards, audit controls, breach notification. **PCI DSS**: contractual standard for card data; minimise scope by never touching card numbers (use a payment provider's hosted fields and tokens); if in scope, twelve requirement areas including network segmentation, encryption, access control, logging and testing. **SOC 2** (not a privacy law but a client expectation): audit of controls including privacy criteria. Part XII chapter 7.

## Working with clients

:::fde
Ask early: what personal data will the system hold, where may it live (region), who are the processors, what retention applies, who handles subject requests, and who is the DPO or legal contact. Put the answers in the data inventory and the threat model. Refuse to copy production data into lower environments or onto laptops, and say why; clients respect it. When a client asks you to do something that looks unlawful (scrape and store data without a basis, retain forever, send PII to an unapproved tool), raise it in writing to their legal function. You are not their lawyer, but you are a professional who notices.
:::

## Common mistakes

- No data inventory; subject requests handled by guesswork.
- PII in logs, analytics and error trackers.
- Production data in staging and on laptops.
- Retention undefined; deletion never implemented.
- Third-party tools (including AI services) added without DPAs or review.
- Collecting fields "in case we need them".
- Region chosen for convenience, violating residency requirements.
- Breach discovered with no logs to scope it.

## Exercises

1. Build the data inventory for a system: every personal data element, store, purpose, retention, processors, access. Tag PII columns in the schema.
2. Implement subject export and erasure as jobs over the inventory, including caches and search; test with a synthetic user.
3. Audit logs, error tracking and analytics for PII; implement redaction and retention; document what remains and why.
