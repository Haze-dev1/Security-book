# Choosing a Database for a Client Project

:::objectives
- Apply a decision framework that starts from the data, the access patterns, the consistency needs, the team and the operations budget, not from technology preferences.
- Evaluate the usual candidates (PostgreSQL/MySQL on RDS or Aurora, DynamoDB, MongoDB/DocumentDB, Redis, OpenSearch, S3-based analytics) against a client's requirements.
- Produce a written recommendation with the reasoning, the alternatives rejected, and the triggers for revisiting it.
:::

:::prereq
All previous chapters in this part; Part III chapter 15; Part VII chapter 11.
:::

## The decision that outlives everything

The database choice is the hardest to reverse: migrations between models take months and risk data. It is also where fashion does the most damage: a document store chosen because the demo was quick, a NoSQL store chosen "for scale" at 50 requests per second, or a relational database chosen by habit for a workload that is genuinely a key-value stream. The framework below forces the questions in the right order and produces a document the client can understand.

## Worked examples

**B2B SaaS for a mid-size client (orders, customers, invoices, 200 tenants, 50 rps peak, reporting needed, EU residency)**: PostgreSQL on RDS Multi-AZ in eu-west-1 with RLS for tenancy, Redis for sessions and caching, S3 for documents; reports from a read replica; no second database. Triggers to revisit: sustained CPU > 60% after optimisation, or a tenant requiring physical isolation (move them to a dedicated instance; Part III chapter 15).

**Event ingestion from IoT devices (100,000 events/second, retained 30 days, queried by device and time, aggregated hourly)**: Kinesis/Kafka → S3 (Parquet, partitioned) for history and Athena; DynamoDB (device ID + time) or Timestream for recent-window queries; PostgreSQL for device metadata and aggregates. Relational alone would need sharding immediately.

**Consumer app with social features (users, posts, follows, feeds, 5M users)**: PostgreSQL for users, posts and relationships (with careful indexing and partitioning of posts by time); Redis for feeds and counters; OpenSearch for search; S3/CloudFront for media. A graph database only if traversal queries dominate and measurements show PostgreSQL recursive CTEs failing.

**Internal tool for 30 staff**: PostgreSQL on a small RDS instance (single-AZ acceptable with the client's written agreement on downtime) or even SQLite on a single VM if truly single-writer; the cost of anything more is unjustified.

**Serverless API with simple records keyed by user (Lambda, spiky traffic)**: DynamoDB with on-demand capacity and TTL; PostgreSQL only if reporting needs emerge, via a stream to S3/Athena.

## Writing the recommendation

An ADR (Part XV chapter 5) with: the requirements (data shape, access patterns, consistency, volumes, team, constraints, budget); the options considered with their fit against each requirement; the recommendation and the reasons; the rejected options and *why* (so nobody re-litigates on fashion); operational implications (backups, HA, monitoring, cost estimate); and **triggers** for revisiting (metrics or requirement changes). A page or two; reviewed by the client's technical owner.

:::fde
Clients sometimes arrive with a decision already made ("we want MongoDB", "it must be Oracle", "our architects said DynamoDB"). Take it seriously: ask what drove it, map it against the framework, and if it fits, build it well. If it does not, show the specific requirement it fails (a reporting need, an integrity rule, a cost) rather than a preference, propose the alternative with its own costs, and record whichever decision they take. Your job is to make the trade-offs visible and the chosen path operable; the client owns the choice.
:::

## Common mistakes

- Choosing by familiarity or fashion without the questions.
- "NoSQL for scale" at volumes a single PostgreSQL instance handles trivially.
- Two or three databases where one plus Redis would do.
- Ignoring the client team's expertise and the hiring market.
- Forgetting residency and compliance until procurement asks.
- No written reasoning; the decision re-argued every quarter.
- No revisit triggers; the system outgrows the choice without anyone noticing.

## Exercises

1. Run the eight questions against a system you know; write the ADR with options, recommendation, rejections and triggers.
2. For a hypothetical client who insists on a document store for relational data, write the one-page note showing the specific requirements it strains and the alternative, in language a non-engineer can follow.
3. Estimate three-year costs (service plus operations hours) for the two most plausible options for a client workload and present them side by side.
