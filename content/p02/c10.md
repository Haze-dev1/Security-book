## The problem

TLS encryption is easy; the hard part is knowing you are encrypting to the *right* party. A certificate is a third party's signed statement: "the holder of this public key controls api.client.com". The system of certificate authorities, trust stores, validation and revocation exists to make that statement trustworthy at Internet scale. Operationally, certificates are also time bombs: they expire, and expired certificates have taken down airlines, payment systems, cloud services and more.

## What is in a certificate

An X.509 certificate contains: the **subject** (historically the Common Name; today the **Subject Alternative Names** extension lists the hostnames, and CN is ignored by browsers); the subject's **public key**; the **issuer** (which CA signed it); **validity** dates (notBefore, notAfter); a **serial number**; **key usage** and **extended key usage** (server auth, client auth); and the issuer's **signature** over all of it.

```bash
openssl x509 -in cert.pem -noout -text | head -40      # everything
openssl x509 -in cert.pem -noout -subject -issuer -dates -ext subjectAltName
```

Validation walks the chain: the leaf's signature is checked with the intermediate's public key, the intermediate's with the root's, and the root must be in the trust store. Plus: names match, dates valid, key usages appropriate, no revocation.

## Validation types

- **Domain Validated (DV)**: the CA checks you control the domain (via HTTP file, DNS record or email). Automated, free (Let's Encrypt, ZeroSSL, ACM), issued in seconds. What nearly everyone uses.
- **Organisation Validated (OV)** and **Extended Validation (EV)**: the CA also verifies the legal entity. Browsers stopped showing EV indicators; practical value is minimal for most services, though some enterprise clients or procurement checklists still ask for OV.
- **Wildcard** (`*.client.com`): covers one level of subdomains; requires DNS validation. Convenient; wider blast radius if the key leaks.

## Getting certificates: ACME

[[acme]] is the protocol Let's Encrypt created to automate issuance. The client proves control of the domain and receives a certificate valid for 90 days, renewing automatically at 60. Short validity is a feature: it forces automation and limits the damage of a leaked key.

**HTTP-01 challenge**: the CA fetches `http://domain/.well-known/acme-challenge/<token>` on port 80. Requires the host to be reachable from the Internet on port 80; cannot issue wildcards.

**DNS-01 challenge**: you publish a TXT record `_acme-challenge.domain`. Works for internal hosts and wildcards; requires API access to your DNS provider (Route 53, Cloudflare), which the client automates.

**TLS-ALPN-01**: validation over port 443 using a special TLS handshake; useful when port 80 is blocked.

```bash
# Caddy: zero configuration. This Caddyfile obtains and renews automatically.
api.client.com {
    reverse_proxy localhost:8000
}

# certbot with Nginx plugin (HTTP-01)
sudo certbot --nginx -d api.client.com -d www.client.com --agree-tos -m ops@client.com
# certbot with Route 53 (DNS-01, wildcard)
sudo certbot certonly --dns-route53 -d 'client.com' -d '*.client.com'
# Renewal is installed as a systemd timer; verify:
systemctl list-timers | grep certbot; sudo certbot renew --dry-run
```

### AWS Certificate Manager

ACM issues free public certificates for use on ALBs, NLBs, CloudFront, API Gateway and App Runner. Validation is by DNS (a CNAME ACM gives you; with Route 53 it is one click) or email. **ACM renews automatically** as long as the validation record stays in place and the certificate is attached to a resource. The private key never leaves AWS and cannot be exported, so ACM public certificates cannot be installed on an EC2 instance's Nginx; for that, use ACME on the instance or ACM Private CA with an exportable cert. CloudFront requires its certificate in `us-east-1` regardless of where your origin is.

## Expiry: the outage you can schedule

Certificates expire at a known second. Yet expiry outages remain common because renewal depended on a person, or the automation broke silently (DNS credentials rotated, port 80 firewalled, a renewal cron removed during a server rebuild), or a certificate lived somewhere nobody remembered (an internal service, a webhook endpoint, a client-provided cert for mTLS).

Defences, all of them:

1. **Automate issuance and renewal** everywhere; no manually installed public certificates.
2. **Monitor expiry from the outside**: a synthetic check that connects to every public endpoint and alerts at 21, 14 and 7 days remaining. Blackbox exporter (Prometheus), Route 53 health checks do not do this, so use a dedicated check or CloudWatch Synthetics canary. ACM emits `DaysToExpiry` metrics; alarm on them.
3. **Monitor the renewal job**, not just the certificate: a failed `certbot renew` should page before the certificate runs out.
4. **Inventory**: a list of every certificate, where it is installed, how it renews, who owns it. Include client-provided certificates and internal ones.
5. **Test renewal** when you build the system (`--dry-run`) and after any change to DNS, firewall or hosting.

Public certificate maximum lifetimes are shrinking by industry decision (398 days today, with a published schedule toward 47 days by 2029). Manual renewal is becoming impossible by design; treat automation as mandatory now.

## Revocation

If a private key leaks, the certificate must be revoked. Mechanisms: **CRLs** (lists downloaded by clients, often stale), **OCSP** (clients ask the CA per certificate; privacy and availability problems), and **OCSP stapling** (the server fetches the OCSP response and includes it in the handshake; enable this). Browsers increasingly rely on their own aggregated revocation lists and short lifetimes rather than live checks. Practical conclusion: revocation is unreliable; short-lived certificates and prompt key rotation are the real control. If a key leaks, revoke *and* reissue *and* deploy immediately.

## Private certificate authorities

Inside a VPC or a client's network you may want TLS between services (ALB to backend, service to database, mTLS between services) with certificates for names that are not public (`orders.internal`, `10.20.64.12`). Options:

- **Self-signed per service**: fine for ALB-to-backend re-encryption because the ALB does not validate; fragile anywhere a client does validate.
- **Private CA**: a root (and intermediates) you control; clients trust it by installing the root. AWS Private CA (ACM PCA) does this as a managed service (notable monthly cost per CA); `step-ca`, HashiCorp Vault PKI, or cert-manager with a CA issuer in Kubernetes are common alternatives. Service meshes run their own.
- **Public certificates for internal names**: possible if the names are under a public domain you control (`orders.internal.client.com`) and you use DNS-01; the certificates appear in public Certificate Transparency logs, revealing hostnames, which may or may not be acceptable.

Operating a private CA means owning: root key protection (HSM or at least offline), intermediate rotation, distribution of the root to every client trust store (base images, config management, mobile devices), and revocation. Do not start one casually; do start one if you need mTLS between many services or encryption to backends that validate.

## Certificate Transparency

Every publicly trusted certificate is logged in public CT logs; browsers require proof of logging. Monitor CT for your domains (free services exist) to detect certificates issued without your knowledge, which indicates DNS compromise or a rogue CA. Set a [[caa]] DNS record so only your chosen CAs may issue.

## Security considerations

- Private keys are the crown jewels: mode 600, owned by root or the service user, never in Git, never emailed. ACM and KMS-backed HSMs remove the key from your hands entirely.
- Key type: ECDSA P-256 keys are smaller and faster than RSA 2048 and universally supported now; RSA remains common for compatibility. Let's Encrypt and ACM offer both.
- A wildcard key on a compromised host lets the attacker impersonate every subdomain; prefer per-service certificates where automation makes it cheap.
- Pinning (hard-coding expected certificates in clients) causes outages on rotation; use CAA and CT monitoring instead, and reserve pinning for high-security mobile apps with a careful rotation plan.

## Common mistakes

- Manual certificate installation on a server, with renewal in someone's calendar.
- Renewal automation that depends on port 80 being open, then a firewall change closes it.
- Certificates in ACM attached to nothing (no auto-renew) or with the validation record deleted.
- Serving the leaf without intermediates (chapter 9).
- CloudFront certificate requested in the wrong region.
- Forgetting internal and client-provided certificates in the inventory.
- Disabling verification in clients because of a self-signed internal certificate instead of distributing the private root.

## Exercises

1. Obtain a certificate for a test domain with Caddy or certbot using DNS-01. Inspect the chain, then run a dry-run renewal.
2. Write a small script that checks the expiry of a list of hostnames and exits non-zero if any is under 14 days. Schedule it or wire it into your monitoring.
3. Build an inventory of every certificate in a system you operate: public, internal, client-provided. For each: where it lives, how it renews, who gets alerted.
