## The problem the Internet solves

In the 1960s and 70s, networks were built by vendors and each spoke its own language: an IBM network could not talk to a DEC network. The research that became the Internet asked a different question: how do you connect *networks of networks* owned by different organisations, running different hardware, with no central operator, so that any machine can reach any other, and so that the system keeps working when parts of it fail?

The answers shaped everything you deploy today:

1. **Packet switching.** Data is chopped into small, independently routed packets rather than reserving a circuit. Links are shared; failures reroute.
2. **Layering.** Each layer provides a service to the one above and relies on the one below, so a web application does not care whether the bytes cross fibre, Wi-Fi or a satellite.
3. **The end-to-end principle.** The network core does one thing (deliver packets, best effort). Reliability, ordering, encryption and application semantics live at the endpoints. This is why your server implements TCP and TLS logic, and a router does not.
4. **Decentralised addressing and routing.** Addresses are allocated hierarchically; routes are exchanged between independent networks by a protocol that assumes no one is in charge.

## Packets

A packet is a bounded chunk of bytes with headers describing where it is going, where it came from, and what it contains. The maximum size a link can carry is its [[mtu]], typically 1,500 bytes on Ethernet. A 100 KB JSON response therefore travels as roughly 70 packets, each of which may take a different path and arrive out of order, duplicated, delayed or not at all. TCP (chapter 6) hides this from your application by numbering and re-sending; UDP does not.

Every packet carries nested headers, one per layer:

## Hosts, switches and routers

- A **host** is any endpoint: your laptop, a server, a phone, a load balancer. Hosts have IP addresses and run applications.
- A **switch** connects hosts on the same local network (Layer 2). It learns which MAC address is on which port and forwards frames accordingly. It does not understand IP.
- A **router** connects different networks (Layer 3). It looks at the destination IP, consults its routing table, and forwards the packet toward the next hop, rewriting the Layer 2 header. Your home router, your VPC's implicit router, and the core routers of an ISP all do this at different scales.
- A **gateway** is simply the router a host sends to when the destination is not on its own network.

In AWS these appear as abstractions: subnets are Layer 2 domains, the VPC router is implicit, an Internet Gateway is a router with NAT for public IPs, and security groups are firewalls attached to each host's virtual interface. Part VII chapter 5 maps them precisely.

## Autonomous systems, ISPs and BGP

The Internet is roughly 75,000 independently operated networks called **autonomous systems** (ASes), each with a number: AWS is AS16509 among others, Google AS15169, Cloudflare AS13335, your ISP has one too. ASes connect to each other through **peering** (exchanging traffic for free, usually at Internet exchange points) and **transit** (paying a larger provider to carry traffic to everyone else).

Routes between ASes are exchanged with [[bgp]], the Border Gateway Protocol. Each AS announces "I can reach 203.0.113.0/24" to its neighbours, who propagate it with their own AS prepended, building paths. Routers choose among paths by policy and length. BGP has no built-in authentication, which is why route leaks and hijacks occur (someone announces a prefix they do not own and traffic flows to them); RPKI is the slow fix being deployed. For an application engineer the practical lessons are:

- Your users' path to you crosses networks you do not control, with latency and loss you do not control. Design for it (chapter 18).
- Major outages are sometimes BGP incidents at a provider, not anything you did. Know how to check (`bgp.tools`, provider status pages) before paging your whole team.
- [[anycast]] lets CDNs and DNS providers announce the same prefix from dozens of locations; BGP routes each user to the nearest. This is how `1.1.1.1` is fast everywhere.

## Why the network is "unreliable" by design

Packets can be lost (buffers overflow on a congested link, radio interference), delayed (queued behind other traffic), reordered (different paths), duplicated (retransmission races), or corrupted (bit errors, usually caught by checksums and discarded). The Internet's core does not fix any of this; it keeps the routers simple and fast and leaves recovery to the endpoints.

Consequences for engineers:

- TCP makes a byte stream look reliable, but at the cost of latency (retransmission timeouts) and throughput (congestion control backs off on loss). A "reliable" connection can stall for seconds.
- A request that timed out may have succeeded on the server. Every operation that can be retried must be [[idempotency|idempotent]] (Part III chapter 3).
- Connections die silently when a middlebox (NAT, firewall) forgets about them. Long-lived connections need keep-alives.
- Partial failure is normal: 0.1% of requests failing somewhere between client and server is a Tuesday, not an incident. Observability must distinguish network noise from application errors.

## The parts you control and the parts you do not

| You control | You influence | You do not control |
|---|---|---|
| Your VPC, subnets, routing, firewalls | Which regions and edge locations you deploy to | Users' ISPs and their peering |
| Your DNS records and TTLs | CDN configuration | BGP decisions outside your AS |
| TLS configuration, certificates | Client timeouts in SDKs you publish | Wi-Fi quality, mobile networks |
| Server-side timeouts, retries, keep-alives | Caching headers that clients honour | Middleboxes on corporate networks |
| Load balancer behaviour | | Submarine cable cuts |

Good architecture puts as much as possible into the first column and designs defensively for the third.

## Real-world context

- **Cloud regions** exist because of physics. A European client's users should be served from a European region, both for latency and for data residency.
- **Outages you will see**: an ISP's BGP misconfiguration taking a country offline for hours (several per year globally); a cloud provider's edge network issue making a region unreachable while your servers are healthy; a certificate expiry at a CDN breaking half the web for an afternoon. Monitoring from outside your network (synthetic checks from several locations) is how you distinguish "we are down" from "the Internet between us and them is down".
- **Corporate networks** add proxies, TLS inspection and aggressive firewalls. Client deployments inside such networks fail in ways that work perfectly from your laptop. Chapter 15 and Part XIII chapter 6.

## Common misconceptions

- "The Internet guarantees delivery." It guarantees nothing; TCP at the endpoints provides reliability.
- "An IP address identifies a person or a device." It identifies an interface at a moment, often shared through NAT by hundreds of people, and reassigned frequently.
- "Latency is a bandwidth problem." Distance sets a floor that bandwidth cannot lower. A 10 Gbps link from Sydney to London still has ~140 ms round trip.
- "If my server is up, the site is up." DNS, BGP, CDN, certificates and the user's network all sit in between.

## Exercises

1. Run `traceroute -n 1.1.1.1` (or `tracert` on Windows) and `traceroute -n` to a server in a distant region. Count hops and compare round-trip times. Identify where the big latency jump occurs (that is usually the ocean).
2. Look up the autonomous system numbers of your ISP and of your cloud provider using a public tool such as `bgp.tools` or `whois`. Find out whether they peer directly.
3. Using `curl -w '%{time_connect} %{time_appconnect} %{time_total}\n' -o /dev/null -s https://example.com`, measure TCP connect, TLS, and total times. Repeat for a site hosted far away. Which component grows most?
