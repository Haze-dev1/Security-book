# The FDE Role and Decision Authority

:::objectives
- Describe what a Forward Deployed Engineer does, how the role differs from a product engineer, a consultant and a solutions architect, and why it exists.
- Understand the decision authority an FDE holds, the decisions that belong to the client or to your own organisation, and how to tell them apart under pressure.
- Adopt the professional stance of the role: owning outcomes in someone else's environment without owning their business.
:::

:::prereq
[What Production-Ready Actually Means](#/p01/c15); Part XII.
:::

## What the role is

A Forward Deployed Engineer takes a product or platform into a specific client's world and makes it work there: integrated with their systems, deployed into their infrastructure or accounts, compliant with their rules, operated by their people or yours, and delivering the outcome they bought. The "forward" is literal: you work at the boundary between your organisation's engineering and the client's reality, often on their premises or in their cloud, with their stakeholders in the room.

This differs from adjacent roles:

| Role | Primary output | Owns | Typical failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product engineer | Features in the core product for all customers | The codebase | Builds what no specific customer needed |
| Solutions architect | Designs and proposals, often pre-sale | The recommendation | Designs are never operated by them |
| Consultant | Advice and deliverables | The engagement | Leaves before production reveals the truth |
| **Forward Deployed Engineer** | A working, secured, operated system at a client | **The outcome in the client's environment** | Becomes a bespoke-everything shop with no product leverage |

The FDE writes code (integration, configuration, extensions, sometimes product changes), builds infrastructure, runs deployments, handles incidents, and spends a surprising share of time understanding people and systems that were never documented. The value you bring is judgement: knowing which of the product's patterns fit this client, which of their constraints are real, and how to get to production without compromising security or sanity.

## The two loyalties

You serve two organisations whose interests mostly align and sometimes do not. The client wants their outcome, cheaply, on their terms. Your organisation wants a successful, reference-able deployment that stays close to the product, is maintainable, profitable and does not create liabilities. The role's core skill is holding both honestly: telling the client when their request creates a risk they do not see; telling your organisation when the product does not fit and bespoke work is the honest answer; and recording both conversations.

Feedback flows in two directions: client realities inform the product roadmap (the same request from five clients is a product gap, not five custom builds), and product constraints bound what you promise the client.

## Decision authority

Most FDE mistakes are decisions made at the wrong level: an engineer silently accepts a security exception that was the client's CISO's to grant, or escalates a trivial configuration choice that was theirs to make. Map the decisions:

Two rules of thumb. First, **you may make a decision more conservative than the baseline without asking; you may never make one less conservative without written acceptance from the owners of the risk.** Second, **if you are unsure whose decision it is, it is not yours alone**: write down the options and ask, briefly and with a recommendation.

## What you are accountable for

Regardless of who decides, you are accountable for: telling the truth about risk, cost and feasibility; following the agreed processes (change, security, access); the quality of what you build; the completeness of what you document; raising concerns through the right path and in writing when you believe a decision is unsafe; and the client's data being handled lawfully and carefully while in your hands. You are not accountable for business decisions the client made with full information, and you protect yourself and your organisation by making sure the information was full and recorded.

:::fde The written record is your professional protection
Decisions, exceptions, accepted risks and refusals belong in email or a shared document, not in a hallway. Not because anyone is dishonest, but because memories differ a year later when something goes wrong. "As agreed on the call, the client accepts running without MFA for the service account until the vendor supports it; risk documented in RISK-14" is a sentence that has saved careers.
:::

## Working in the client's environment

Practical norms:

- **Their rules apply** in their environment: access processes, change windows, data classification, acceptable use, security tooling. Learn them in discovery (chapter 2); do not assume your organisation's norms transfer.
- **Least privilege for yourself**: ask for the access you need, time-bound, through their process; refuse shared credentials; never use personal accounts.
- **Their data stays theirs**: no copies to your laptop, your cloud, your chat tools; production data never used for development; chapter 7 covers the details.
- **Visibility**: the client should be able to see everything you do in their environment (CloudTrail, audit logs, a shared channel); if you would not want it logged, do not do it.
- **Respect their people**: their IT team may feel threatened by an outsider deploying into their world; make them collaborators and credit them.
- **Leave it better documented than you found it**, including their own systems where you learned things nobody had written down.

## The professional stance

The FDE's temperament: calm under production pressure, curious about systems and people, honest about uncertainty, firm on security and process, flexible on everything that does not matter, and allergic to heroics (a system that needs you to survive is a system you failed to finish). You say "I don't know yet; I will find out by Thursday" without embarrassment, "no, and here is why, and here is what we can do instead" without apology, and "I was wrong; here is the fix" without delay.

## Common mistakes

- Accepting security exceptions or scope changes informally to be helpful.
- Escalating everything, so that nobody reads the escalations.
- Treating the client's IT team as an obstacle rather than the future operators.
- Building bespoke solutions when the product (or a product change) would serve everyone.
- Keeping decisions in chat and memory.
- Confusing responsiveness with availability: being reachable is professional; being the single point of failure is not.

## Exercises

1. For a current or past engagement, list ten decisions made in the last month and assign each to its rightful owner using the table; identify any made at the wrong level.
2. Write the email documenting a security exception request you have seen (or invent one), including risk, alternatives, and the acceptance you need.
3. Draft a one-page "how I work in your environment" note for a new client covering access, data, visibility, change process and communication.
