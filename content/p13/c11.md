# Production Readiness Reviews and Rollback Plans

:::objectives
- Run a production readiness review (PRR) that decides go/no-go on evidence, before the first production deployment and before major changes.
- Write a rollback plan for every launch and significant change that is specific, tested and time-bounded.
- Handle the pressure of a launch date honestly: what can be deferred, what cannot, and how to say so.
:::

[What Production-Ready Actually Means](#/p01/c15); [Production Change Management](#/p12/c08); [Deployment Strategies: Rolling, Blue-Green, Canary and Feature Flags](#/p10/c07).

## What a PRR is

A production readiness review is a structured check, by people who did not build the system, that it can be operated safely in production: it is secure, observable, recoverable, documented, supportable and compliant with the client's requirements. It produces a decision (go, go with conditions, no-go), a list of gaps with owners and dates, and the evidence that the decision was made deliberately. It is not a demo and not a design review; those happened earlier.

Run it two to three weeks before the planned go-live so there is time to fix findings; repeat a short version before major releases, Region expansions and ownership transfers.

## The review checklist

The full checklist is in Part XV chapter 1; the categories and the questions a reviewer insists on evidence for:

```checklist
# Production readiness: evidence required
## Security
- Threat model exists and mitigations implemented; pen-test findings at high+ closed or accepted in writing
- Authentication via the agreed IdP; authorisation tests (incl. cross-tenant) in CI and green
- Secrets in the secret store; no credentials in code, config or logs (scan results)
- Encryption at rest and in transit everywhere; KMS key policies reviewed
- Dependency and image scans clean of critical/high (or accepted exceptions with expiry)
- Audit logging of security-relevant events shipped to the client's SIEM
- WAF and rate limits on public endpoints; security headers verified
## Reliability
- SLOs defined with SLIs measured at the edge; dashboards exist
- Multi-AZ for every stateful component; no single points of failure without accepted risk
- Health checks correct (liveness vs readiness); graceful shutdown; autoscaling bounds tested
- Dependency failure modes tested (timeouts, breakers, degradation)
- Load test at expected peak × headroom passed; quotas raised
## Operability
- Alerts for every SLO and critical failure with runbooks linked; routed to a staffed destination
- On-call and escalation defined, including the client's role
- Deployment via pipeline with rollback tested; deployment records produced
- Logs structured, retained per policy, free of PII and secrets (sampled)
- Cost budget and alarms set; tags applied
## Recoverability
- Backups automated, isolated, encrypted; a restore performed and timed within the last 30 days
- RTO/RPO documented and demonstrated for the agreed disaster scenarios
- Rebuild guide tested
## Documentation and ownership
- Environment document, runbooks, configuration reference, architecture and ADRs current
- Service ownership record signed; support model and contacts agreed
- Risk register with accepted risks signed by the client
## Compliance and data
- Data map and retention; deletion and export paths working; DPA and vendor agreements in place
- Change management process agreed with the client and evidence capture working
```

Each line has three possible states: evidence seen (link), gap with owner and date, or accepted risk with signatory. "We will do it after launch" is a gap with a date, not a pass.

## Running the review

- **Reviewers**: a senior engineer not on the build team, a security representative, the operations owner (yours or the client's), and the client's technical lead; the build team presents evidence and does not grade itself.
- **Evidence first**: the build team fills the checklist with links before the meeting; the meeting discusses gaps and risks, not the items with evidence.
- **Decision rules agreed in advance**: any open critical security gap is a no-go; any missing backup restore is a no-go; gaps in lower categories may be conditions with dates.
- **Record**: the completed checklist, the decision, the conditions, the sign-offs, stored with the project and given to the client.

Reviews find the same things repeatedly: alerts routed nowhere, backups never restored, a secret in a task definition, no load test, runbooks missing the rollback step, nobody owning the on-call rota. Expect them; schedule time to fix them.

## Rollback plans

Every launch and every significant change needs a written rollback plan, decided before execution:

```checklist
# Rollback plan
- Trigger criteria: the specific, observable signals that mean roll back (SLO burn, error ratio, failed verification, customer-impacting defect), and who decides (named role)
- Decision window: how long after deployment the rollback remains the default response (e.g. 2 hours of hypercare), after which forward-fix is preferred
- Application rollback: exact mechanism (redeploy previous digest / switch target group / flip flag), command or pipeline action, expected duration, who executes
- Data considerations: are there schema or data changes? Are they expand-only (safe to roll back the app)? If not, what is the data recovery path and how long does it take?
- Dependencies: what else changed at the same time (DNS, config, integrations, third-party settings) and how each reverts
- Verification after rollback: dashboards, probes, smoke tests to confirm recovery
- Communication: who is told, status page wording prepared in advance
- Tested: when and where this rollback was rehearsed (staging, game day)
- Point of no return: any step after which rollback is no longer possible (data migration cutover, DNS with long TTLs, external announcements) and the explicit go/no-go before it
```

For launches that migrate from an old system, plan the **cutover** as a sequence with checkpoints: dual-run period, data sync and validation, traffic switch (gradual where possible), old system kept in read-only standby for the rollback window, and a decommission decision only after the window. The old system is the rollback; do not switch it off on day one.

## Launch day

A launch is a high-risk change (Part XII chapter 8): a plan with steps, owners, times and abort criteria; a bridge or channel with the client; monitoring watched actively; the rollback plan open on screen; stakeholder updates on schedule; a hypercare period with heightened on-call and daily check-ins; and a launch retrospective a week later. Launch mid-week, mid-morning, with everyone needed available for the day and the following day.

## When the date is fixed and the system is not ready

Launch dates have real drivers: contracts, regulations, marketing, the old system's expiry. When the PRR finds gaps against a fixed date:

- Separate **must-fix** (security critical, no backup, no rollback, no monitoring) from **should-fix** (lower-severity items that can follow with dates). The must-fix list is not negotiable; say so plainly and early, with the specific risk of each item.
- Offer **scope reduction**: launch to a pilot group, a subset of features behind flags, read-only mode, or a soft launch without marketing, which reduces blast radius while gaps close.
- Offer **compensating controls** for the short term: manual monitoring shifts, extra approvals, tighter rate limits.
- Put every residual risk in writing with acceptance by the client's risk owner and your leadership (chapter 1).
- Never fix the date by dropping the must-fix list quietly. If the decision-makers accept the risk with full information, that is their decision; if they are not given the information, it is your failure.

:::fde "The regulator's deadline is Friday"
Then the conversation is: here is what is ready, here is what is not, here are the three options (launch reduced scope Friday; launch full scope with these specific risks accepted in writing and these compensating controls; negotiate with the regulator with this evidence of progress). Present them Monday, not Thursday. Fixed deadlines are managed by early, honest options, never by hoping the gaps do not matter.
:::

## Common mistakes

- A PRR as a presentation by the build team with no independent reviewers.
- Passing items on promises instead of evidence.
- Rollback plans that say "redeploy previous version" without the digest, the data path or a test.
- Switching off the old system on launch day.
- Launching on Friday afternoon.
- Accepting a fixed date by silently dropping security and recovery items.

## Exercises

1. Run a PRR on a system you know using the checklist; collect evidence links for each item; classify gaps and write the decision record.
2. Write and rehearse a rollback plan for a release that includes a schema change; identify the point of no return and the data path.
3. Prepare the three-option memo for a fixed-date launch with two must-fix gaps; present it to a colleague as the sponsor.
