# Technical Handovers

:::objectives
- Execute the technical side of a handover: knowledge transfer, access transfer, operational rehearsal and acceptance, so the receiving team can run the system without you.
- Produce the handover package and prove it works before signing off.
- Handle the human side: receiving teams who are overloaded, sceptical or under-skilled, and your own team's wish to be done.
:::

:::prereq
[Client Handovers, Operational Ownership and Professional Incident Management](#/p12/c10); [Environment Configuration and Deployment Documentation](#/p13/c10).
:::

## The handover standard

A technical handover is complete when the receiving team has **demonstrated**, not been told, that they can: deploy a change and roll it back; respond to each paging alert using the runbook; rotate every secret; restore from backup and verify; rebuild an environment from the guide; grant and revoke access; understand the architecture well enough to make a change safely; reach every integration owner and vendor; and know the open risks. Everything else (documents, recordings, sessions) exists to produce that demonstration.

## The handover package

Assembled over the final weeks, stored where the client owns it:

```checklist
# Technical handover package
- Architecture: diagrams (as code), ADRs, design documents, integration contracts
- Environment documents per environment (chapter 10) with last-verified dates
- Operations: runbooks per alert and procedure; deployment runbook; rebuild guide; on-call guide; incident process integration
- Configuration reference and client manifest
- Source code and IaC repositories transferred or mirrored to the client's organisation; pipelines running in their CI or documented for migration; branch protections and CODEOWNERS updated to their team
- Credentials and access inventory: every secret, key, role, account, with owner and rotation schedule; your access listed for removal
- Systems and contacts register: every integration and vendor, contacts, escalation paths, contract references
- Monitoring: dashboards exported or provisioned in their tooling; alert rules in code; alert destinations changed to their rota
- Backups: schedule, locations, restore procedure, last restore test report
- Cost: current spend, forecast, budget alarms, tags, reserved commitments and their expiry
- Risk register and technical debt register with recommendations and priorities
- Version matrix: versions of every component, end-of-life dates, upgrade recommendations
- Maintenance calendar: patching, dependency updates, certificate renewals, access reviews, restore drills, with owners
- Training record and session recordings
- Support agreement and escalation path for after handover
- Acceptance checklist (Part XV chapter 6) signed by both sides
```

## Knowledge transfer that works

Lectures transfer little. Structure the transfer as **doing with decreasing support**:

1. **Walkthroughs** (week 1): architecture, environments, codebase tour, operational tooling; recorded; the receiving team asks questions and you write every question into the documentation as an improvement.
2. **Shadowing** (weeks 1–2): they watch you deploy, respond to an alert, perform maintenance; you narrate why, not just what.
3. **Reverse shadowing** (weeks 2–3): they perform each procedure from the documentation while you watch silently; you intervene only for safety; every stumble is a documentation fix made that day.
4. **Supervised independence** (hypercare, weeks 4–8): they operate; you are reachable with agreed response times; weekly review of questions and incidents; the question count trending to zero is the signal.

Prioritise by risk: deploy and rollback, incident response for the top alerts, restore, secret rotation first; nice-to-know architecture history last.

Game days during reverse shadowing are the most effective single technique: break something in staging (kill the database, expire a certificate, push a bad deploy) and let them run the response with the runbook. Their performance is the handover's acceptance test, and what they struggle with is your remaining work.

## Access transfer

Transfer, do not share. Ownership of repositories, registries, DNS, cloud accounts, vendor consoles and SaaS admin moves to client identities; your identities are removed on a published date; the client verifies removal (and so do you: attempt to use the old access and confirm failure). Every secret you ever handled is rotated during handover, so nothing you knew remains valid. Document the final state in the credentials inventory and sign it.

## When the receiving team is not ready

Common and awkward: the team is small, overloaded, lacks cloud or container experience, or does not want the system. Options, discussed with both sponsors early rather than at the end:

- **Extend hypercare** with defined scope and price.
- **Managed service or hybrid support** (Part XII chapter 10) for a period, with a capability-building plan and exit criteria.
- **Targeted training** before handover on the specific skills the system requires (Terraform, ECS, the observability stack), scheduled weeks ahead.
- **Simplify** what is handed over where possible: fewer moving parts beats more documentation.
- **Document the capability gap as a risk** accepted by the client if they choose to proceed anyway, with the consequences spelled out (slower incident response, patching lag).

Never hand over to a team that has not demonstrated the core procedures and call it done; the system will fail in months and both organisations will own that failure.

## Your own team's exit

Engineers finishing an engagement want to move on; the last weeks get the least attention and matter most. Protect the handover phase in the plan with named people and no parallel start on the next project for the handover lead; make the acceptance checklist the definition of done; and hold a short internal retrospective: what would have made this handover easier if done from day one (usually: documentation as you go, pipelines from the start, no personal access paths).

:::fde Handover is where the client decides whether to call you again
A system that keeps running smoothly after you leave, operated by their team with your documentation, is the most persuasive sales asset your organisation has. A system that collapses two months later erases a year of good work. Treat the handover with the seriousness of the launch.
:::

## Common mistakes

- Handover as a document dump in the final week.
- Knowledge transfer by lecture; no reverse shadowing; no game day.
- Access shared rather than transferred; your role still active a year later.
- Secrets not rotated at exit.
- Signing off a team that has not demonstrated restore or rollback.
- The handover lead already on the next project.

## Exercises

1. Build the handover package list for a real system and mark each item as exists, needs work, or missing; estimate the work.
2. Design a game day for a receiving team covering a bad deploy, a certificate expiry and a database failover; run it and log documentation fixes.
3. Write the access transfer plan: every identity and credential, the transfer action, the date, the verifier, and the rotation list.
