# Deploying into Client-Owned Cloud Accounts

:::objectives
- Deploy and operate your system inside an AWS account the client owns, within their landing zone, guardrails and processes.
- Set up the account relationship correctly: access, pipelines, state, registries, secrets, logging and cost, without taking ownership the client should keep.
- Handle the recurring frictions: service control policies, missing permissions, approved-service lists, egress restrictions and shared responsibility gaps.
:::

## Why clients want it in their account

Ownership of data and infrastructure, residency and compliance evidence in their own logs, use of their existing security tooling and contracts, cost visibility on their bill, and the ability to end the relationship without losing the system. For you it means every client is a separate deployment target with its own quirks; the product and pipeline must be built for that from the start.

## Account setup

Ideally the client vends accounts from their landing zone (Control Tower or equivalent) with their baseline applied: organisation CloudTrail, Config, GuardDuty, SCP guardrails, centralised logging, network connectivity, SSO. You request:

- **Accounts per environment** (at minimum production and non-production; ideally dev, staging, production) in their organisation units for workloads of this type.
- **Identity**: roles for your pipeline (OIDC trust to your CI, or their CI), roles for your engineers via their SSO with permission sets per environment (chapter 7), break-glass procedure.
- **Network**: VPCs provisioned by them or by your IaC within their IP plan; connectivity to their shared services (DNS, proxies, on-premises) as needed; egress rules for the destinations your system needs (package registries, telemetry endpoints, third-party APIs), requested with justification.
- **Logging**: your CloudTrail and application logs flowing to their central log archive and SIEM per their standard; you may also need your own copies for support, agreed in the contract.
- **Registry and artefacts**: either you push images into an ECR in their account (replication or pipeline push) or they pull from your registry with a cross-account policy; the former keeps their production independent of your registry's availability.
- **Secrets**: their Secrets Manager/Parameter Store in each account; your application reads from there.
- **DNS and certificates**: zones they own; delegation of a subdomain to a zone in the workload account is often the cleanest; ACM certificates in the account.
- **Cost**: tags per your agreed scheme; budget alarms; a cost report they receive.

Document the setup in the environment configuration document (chapter 10) as you go.

## Pipelines across many accounts

Your delivery pipeline (Part X) becomes a fan-out: one build producing a signed artefact, promoted to each client's environments in waves (canary clients first), with per-client configuration held in a per-client repository or directory, per-client approval rules (some clients require their own approval for production), and per-client deployment records delivered to them. Options for the pipeline's location:

- **Your CI with OIDC roles in each client account**: simplest for you; the client sees the role's actions in their CloudTrail; the trust policy binds to your repository and environment. Some clients refuse external pipelines touching production.
- **The client's CI (their GitHub/GitLab/CodePipeline)** running your pipeline definitions: satisfies "nothing external deploys to our production"; you need a path to contribute pipeline code and artefacts; upgrades need their cooperation.
- **A deployment agent inside their account** (GitOps with Argo CD or Flux pulling from a repository you publish to; or a Systems Manager/Step Functions workflow they trigger): the client's environment pulls changes; no inbound access needed; well suited to Kubernetes-based products.

Terraform state per client per environment, in the client's account (an S3 backend with locking in a tooling account of theirs), so they own their infrastructure definition and can apply it without you.

Other recurring points:

- **Approved services**: if your design uses a service they have not approved, you need their exception process (weeks) or a substitute; discover early.
- **Permissions you lack**: ask for the specific actions with justification rather than a wildcard; platform teams respond better to precise requests and you keep least privilege.
- **Their tooling agents**: EDR, log forwarders, backup agents may be mandatory on compute; containers on Fargate may need alternative controls agreed with security.
- **KMS**: keys may be created only by their key administrators; your IaC references key ARNs as variables.
- **Shared services**: DNS resolvers, proxies, NTP, package mirrors; your system must be configurable to use them.
- **Quotas**: new accounts have default quotas; request increases before load testing.

## Shared responsibility, written down

Agree a RACI for the deployment: who patches base images; who rotates which secrets; who monitors what; who responds to GuardDuty findings in the account; who pays for what; who approves changes; who owns the backup vault; who answers the auditor. The cloud provider's shared responsibility model stops at the service line; the line between you and the client is the one that causes disputes, so write it in the contract and the environment documentation.

## Upgrades and support across clients

N clients means N production environments at potentially different versions. Keep a version matrix; limit supported versions (current and previous); roll upgrades in waves with client-specific windows; automate configuration drift detection per client; give each client a deployment record and release notes; and plan for the client who freezes changes for a quarter.

:::fde Build for the client-owned model even if your first clients are hosted by you
Parameterised IaC, pipelines that fan out, no dependency on your own infrastructure at runtime (registries, secret stores, identity), and documentation that stands alone are far cheaper to build in from the start than to retrofit when the first enterprise client insists on their own account.
:::

## Common mistakes

- Deploying from a laptop into a client's production because the pipeline was not ready.
- Production depending on your registry, your secret store or your identity provider.
- Terraform state in your account for their infrastructure.
- Discovering SCPs and approved-service lists on deployment day.
- No RACI; both sides assume the other is patching.
- Treating every client environment as unique instead of as parameters to one pipeline.
