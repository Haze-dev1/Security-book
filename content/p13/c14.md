# Communicating Technical Tradeoffs to Non-Technical Stakeholders

:::objectives
- Explain technical trade-offs so that business stakeholders can make the decisions that are theirs, with real understanding of consequences.
- Structure the conversation: the decision, the options, the consequences in their terms, the recommendation, and what you need from them.
- Handle the hard versions: cost, risk, delays, security findings and "why can't we just".
:::

:::prereq
[Documentation and Stakeholder Communication](#/p12/c09); [The FDE Role and Decision Authority](#/p13/c01); [Choosing a Hosting Architecture and Estimating Costs](#/p13/c05).
:::

## The goal is a good decision, not a technical education

Stakeholders do not need to understand Multi-AZ; they need to decide whether 35 USD a month is worth avoiding four hours of downtime a year. Your job is to translate a technical choice into the consequences they care about (money, time, risk, customer experience, compliance, people), present honest options, recommend, and let them decide what is theirs. If they understand the consequences and choose differently from your recommendation, the communication succeeded.

## Structure every trade-off conversation

1. **The decision in one sentence**: "We need to decide whether the portal runs in one data centre or two."
2. **Why it matters to them**: "It determines how long the portal is down if a data centre fails, and the monthly cost."
3. **Options (two or three)**, each with: what it means for them, cost, risk, time, and what it rules out later. Same dimensions for every option, in a table.
4. **Recommendation and why**, tied to their stated priorities.
5. **What you need**: the decision, by when, and what happens if it is delayed.
6. **Confirm understanding**: ask them to restate the choice; correct gently.
7. **Record** the decision and the reasoning (ADR or email) and send it back to them.

## Translating technical concepts

Anchor in their world and quantify:

| Instead of | Say |
|---|---|
| "We need Multi-AZ RDS" | "A second copy of the database in another building that takes over automatically; 35 USD a month; avoids hours of downtime" |
| "The API lacks idempotency" | "If a payment request is retried after a network hiccup, the customer could be charged twice; we need a safeguard; two days of work" |
| "Technical debt in the orders module" | "Changes to anything involving orders take three times longer than they should and have caused two of the last five incidents; a two-week investment would fix that" |
| "No RLS on the shared database" | "Right now, one programming mistake could show one of your customers another customer's invoices; this control makes that impossible even if a mistake happens" |
| "We should not use Kubernetes" | "That option needs a specialist your team does not have and costs more than the whole system; the simpler option does what you need" |
| "p99 latency is 2 seconds" | "One in a hundred page loads takes over two seconds; on the checkout page that means lost sales" |
| "The pen test found a high" | "Someone with a customer login could download other customers' documents by changing a number in the address; we must fix this before launch; one day" |

Analogies help once, then switch to the real numbers. Avoid acronyms, or define them the first time and then use them consistently. Check for understanding by listening to how they restate it; if they say "so we're buying a backup", gently correct ("a backup is for recovering lost data; this is a standby that keeps running").

## Cost conversations

Present total cost of ownership, not the cheapest line: infrastructure, licences, support, the client's own people's time, and the cost of incidents at their stated downtime value. Make trade-offs money-to-money where possible ("spending X avoids an expected Y per year"). When they push on cost, show the levers and their consequences rather than defending the number: "we can halve the database cost by accepting manual recovery, which means a 2-hour outage a few times a year handled by your IT generalist on call; your choice." Separate one-off and recurring costs. Say what is uncertain and when it will firm up.

## Risk conversations

Risk is likelihood times impact in their terms. "There is a realistic chance (we see attempts weekly) that an attacker guesses a weak staff password; without MFA that gives them every customer record; the fix is a day of configuration and a change for your staff's login." Avoid both fear and false comfort; give the facts, the mitigation, the cost, and your recommendation. For residual risks they choose to accept, write the acceptance with the consequence spelled out and get it signed; this is kindness, not bureaucracy, because it means they decided knowingly.

## Delays and bad news

State the fact in the first sentence; the cause in the second; the options in the third. "The go-live will slip two weeks. The accounting vendor's test environment arrived three weeks late and we found their API behaves differently from the documentation. Options: launch on time without invoice sync (manual entry for two weeks), launch in two weeks complete, or launch on time with sync in read-only mode. I recommend the third." No burying the news in paragraph four; no blaming; no defensiveness; a recommendation. Deliver by voice, confirm in writing.

## "Why can't we just…"

The question is reasonable; the answer must be respectful and concrete. "Why can't we just skip the staging environment?" "We can, and it saves about 60 USD a month. The trade-off is that every change is tested for the first time on your live system with your customers watching; the last two incidents at similar clients came from exactly that. I recommend keeping it; if cost is the concern, we can run it only during working hours and halve that." Give the real consequence, quantify, respect the question, recommend. If the answer is genuinely "we could, and it is fine", say so; credibility comes from not defending every technical preference as essential.

## Reading the room

Different stakeholders want different framings: the CFO wants cost and risk in money; the COO wants operational impact and dates; the CISO wants controls and evidence; the sponsor wants to know whether they will look good. Prepare the same facts in each framing. Watch for the nod that means "I have stopped understanding but do not want to say so"; slow down, ask a question that lets them show understanding without embarrassment ("which of these would you be most uncomfortable explaining to your board?").

:::fde Your credibility is the asset
Stakeholders cannot evaluate your technical claims; they evaluate you. Credibility comes from: numbers that turn out right, bad news delivered early, recommendations that sometimes cost you work ("you don't need this"), admitting uncertainty, and never being caught overstating. Spend it carefully and it compounds; overspend it once and every later recommendation is discounted.
:::

## Common mistakes

- Explaining the technology instead of the consequence.
- One option presented as the only option.
- Point estimates with false precision; or hedging into meaninglessness.
- Burying bad news.
- Defending every technical preference as a requirement.
- Not recording the decision and the reasoning.
- Jargon with no definitions, or analogies that never give way to numbers.

## Exercises

1. Take a technical decision from a current project and write the seven-step structure for a non-technical sponsor, including the options table in their terms.
2. Translate ten technical findings or recommendations from your work into consequence statements with numbers.
3. Role-play delivering a slip and a security finding to a colleague playing a sceptical CFO; ask them afterwards what they understood and what they would decide.
