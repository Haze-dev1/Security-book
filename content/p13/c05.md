# Choosing a Hosting Architecture and Estimating Costs

:::objectives
- Select a hosting architecture for a client from their constraints (ownership, skills, compliance, budget, scale, availability) rather than from fashion.
- Produce a credible monthly cost estimate with assumptions, ranges and the levers that change it, and present it with the trade-offs.
- Avoid the classic errors: over-engineering for scale that never comes, under-engineering operations, and forgetting the costs that are not compute.
:::

:::prereq
Part VII, especially [Billing, Tagging, Budgets and Cost Monitoring](#/p07/c04); Part IX chapters 1, 10, 17 and 18; [Scoping and Estimation](#/p12/c02).
:::

## Decide from constraints

The right architecture is the simplest one that meets the client's constraints with margin. Collect them explicitly:

| Constraint | Question | Pushes toward |
|---|---|---|
| Ownership | Must it run in the client's account? Must they own the IaC and data? | Client-account deployment; portable IaC |
| Operating capability | Who runs it, with what skills and hours? | Managed services; fewer moving parts; the smallest operational surface |
| Compliance and residency | Which regulations, which Regions, which certifications? | Specific Regions; private networking; certain services only |
| Availability | Business hours or 24×7? What does an hour of downtime cost? | Multi-AZ at minimum; multi-Region only when the cost of downtime justifies it |
| Scale | Current and 12-month expected load; peaks | Right-sized instances; autoscaling; serverless for spiky low-volume |
| Integration | Private connectivity to on-premises? Latency to systems of record? | Region choice; VPN/Direct Connect; placement |
| Budget | Build and run envelopes | The whole shape |
| Existing platform | Landing zone, approved services, mandated tooling | Fit their standards even when yours differ |
| Exit | Could they move it to another provider or team? | Standard components; documented; no exotic lock-in |

Then choose among a small set of proven shapes:

## Estimating cost

A credible estimate lists every component, its unit price, the quantity driven by stated assumptions, and the resulting monthly figure, then sums with a contingency and shows the range:


Walk through the categories; the ones people forget are in bold:

- Compute: instances or tasks × hours; Lambda invocations and GB-seconds; include non-production environments and their schedules (staging off at night saves 60%).
- Database: instance class × hours (Multi-AZ doubles), storage, IOPS, backups beyond free allowance, read replicas.
- Load balancing and networking: ALB hours and LCUs; **NAT Gateway hours and per-GB processing** (often the surprise line); **data transfer out** (to the internet, cross-Region, cross-AZ); VPN or Direct Connect.
- Storage: S3 by class, requests, lifecycle; EBS volumes and snapshots; **backup vaults and cross-Region copies**.
- Edge: CloudFront requests and transfer; WAF rules and requests; Route 53 zones and queries; ACM is free.
- Security and operations: KMS keys and requests; Secrets Manager per secret; CloudTrail (one trail free, more and data events cost); GuardDuty, Security Hub, Config rules; **CloudWatch logs ingestion and retention, custom metrics, alarms, dashboards**; Systems Manager.
- Messaging: SQS/SNS requests; EventBridge; Kinesis/MSK if used.
- Email/SMS: SES per message; SNS SMS is expensive per message.
- Third parties: identity provider seats, monitoring SaaS, error tracking, payment provider fees.
- **Support plan**: Business support is 10% of spend (minimum 100 USD) and often necessary for production.
- **People**: operations hours, on-call, patching, the support agreement; often larger than the infrastructure bill and always part of the client's total cost.

Use the AWS Pricing Calculator for the line items, then sanity-check against real bills from similar deployments (keep your own reference library). State assumptions (requests/day, data growth, Region, reserved versus on-demand) next to the numbers, give a range (−20% / +40% is honest at design stage), and name the top three levers (NAT architecture, database size, log retention) so the client knows what moves the bill.

## Cost-aware design choices

- Right-size from measurements, then let autoscaling handle peaks; do not provision for the hoped-for launch day permanently.
- Graviton (arm64) instances where the stack supports it: typically 20% cheaper per performance.
- VPC endpoints for S3, DynamoDB, ECR, Secrets Manager, CloudWatch to avoid NAT processing charges; a single NAT in staging; NAT per AZ only in production where the availability justifies it.
- Schedules for non-production environments.
- Storage lifecycle policies and log retention set at creation, not after the first bill.
- Reserved Instances or Savings Plans once the baseline is known (after two or three months of production).
- Budgets and anomaly detection from day one, with alerts to the client (Part VII chapter 4).
- Tag everything with project, environment and owner so the bill is attributable.

## Presenting the decision

Give the client two or three options with the same table (shape, what it gives, operations required, monthly cost range, risks) and a recommendation with reasons tied to their constraints. Include the cost of *not* doing something ("single-AZ saves 35 USD a month and costs an estimated 4 hours of downtime a year with manual recovery, at your stated 2,000 USD per hour of downtime"). Record the decision as an ADR. Revisit when constraints change: growth, new compliance requirements, a new operating team.

:::fde "Can't we just use the free tier?"
Sometimes, for pilots. For production, explain that the free tier is for learning, that minimum production hygiene (Multi-AZ database, backups, WAF, logging, alarms, support plan) has a floor around 150–250 USD per month on AWS regardless of traffic, and that the alternative is a PaaS with a lower floor and less ownership. Then show the three-line comparison. Most clients choose the floor once the trade-offs are concrete.
:::

## Common mistakes

- Choosing the architecture before the constraints.
- Forgetting NAT, data transfer, logs, backups, support plans and people.
- Point estimates without assumptions or ranges.
- Over-provisioning "for launch" and never scaling down.
- Building for a load that is 100× the realistic 12-month forecast.
- Not setting budgets and alarms at deployment time.
- A design the client's team cannot operate.
