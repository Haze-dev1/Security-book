# Troubleshooting Production Incidents at a Client

:::objectives
- Diagnose incidents in environments you do not fully control, with partial access, unfamiliar tooling and the client's people in the room.
- Apply a systematic method that works under pressure: establish impact, timeline and change; isolate the layer; test hypotheses cheaply; mitigate reversibly.
- Manage the client relationship during the incident: roles, communication, boundaries and the decisions that are theirs.
:::

## What is different at a client

You may not have access to everything (their network team owns the firewall; their DBA owns the database); the tooling is theirs (a SIEM you have never used, dashboards built by someone else); several organisations may be involved (their IT, your team, a vendor, a cloud provider); the process is theirs (their severity scale, their bridge, their change approvals even during incidents); and the people watching include the sponsor who signed the contract. The engineering method is the same; the orchestration is harder.

## Working with partial access

- **Ask for exactly what you need, now**: "I need read access to the ALB access logs and the RDS Performance Insights for prod-orders for the next four hours." Specific requests get approved during incidents; vague ones do not.
- **Drive by proxy**: when the client's engineer has the access, you direct: "run this exact command and paste the output." Prepare the commands; read the output with them.
- **Use what the application tells you**: your own logs, traces and metrics are the parts you control; design them so that an incident at a client can be diagnosed from them alone as far as possible (dependency latencies, error classification, configuration fingerprint, version).
- **Reproduce from outside**: `curl` with verbose output, `dig`, `openssl s_client`, traceroute from where the users are (their office, the internet, inside the VPC); the client's network team will often need your evidence to look at their side.
- **Know the owners**: the systems and contacts register from discovery (chapter 2) is what you open first; the incident is not the time to find out who runs DNS.

## Frequent client-side causes

| Symptom | Common client-side cause | Evidence to ask for |
|---|---|---|
| Sudden total failure, your logs quiet | Firewall or proxy rule change; certificate expiry at their edge; DNS change | Edge logs; their change calendar; `openssl s_client` from outside |
| Intermittent timeouts to an integration | Their system's maintenance; rate limit; a load balancer with a shorter idle timeout than your keep-alive | Their system's status; adapter metrics; TCP resets in logs |
| Authentication failures for everyone | IdP certificate rotation; group changes; conditional access policy change | IdP logs; token claims comparison |
| Slow responses on their network only | Proxy inspection; MTU/fragmentation over VPN; DNS resolution latency | Trace from inside their network; `mtr`; DNS timing |
| Permissions errors after a quiet period | Credential rotation not propagated; SCP or IAM change by their platform team | CloudTrail for the denied call; Secrets Manager version history |
| Data wrong, system healthy | Upstream data change; a batch job on their side; time zone or encoding change | Source record comparison; reconciliation report |

Say "a change on your side" factually and without blame; it is usually true and the team will tell you more if they are not being accused.

## Managing the room

Agree the roles at the start even if it takes 60 seconds: who is the incident commander (yours or theirs per the contract), who speaks to the client's stakeholders, who is hands-on. Keep one channel and one timeline both sides can see. Give updates on the cadence the client's process expects. Stay inside your authority: you may roll back your deployment; you may not restart their database without the DBA; you present the trade-off, they decide. If the client's team is making it worse (restarting everything, changing several things at once), say so politely and specifically through the commander: "Can we hold other changes for ten minutes while we test one hypothesis? Otherwise we cannot tell what helped."

## Afterwards

The postmortem (Part XI chapter 7) is joint, blameless and includes the client's contributing factors as plainly as yours, agreed before publication. The incident report to the client follows the contract's timeline. Action items on their side are tracked too, with their owners; follow up at the next review. Update your runbooks and the systems register with what you learned about their environment; the next incident will be faster.

:::fde The incident is also an audit of your discovery
Every "we didn't know they had a proxy" during an incident is a discovery gap. Note them. The register, the environment document and the integration profiles improve with every incident, and the client sees you learning their environment rather than repeating questions.
:::

## Common mistakes

- Diagnosing before establishing impact and start time.
- Not asking the client's teams what changed.
- Changing several things at once across two organisations.
- Taking actions outside your authority in their environment "to be fast".
- Updating stakeholders only when there is good news.
- Not recording the timeline because everyone is busy.

## Exercises

1. Run a tabletop incident with a colleague playing the client's network engineer who has access you lack; practise precise asks and evidence sharing.
2. Review your application's logs, traces and metrics: could you diagnose a dependency timeout, a configuration mismatch and a version regression from them alone, without infrastructure access? Add what is missing.
3. Write the client-side-cause table for your product's typical deployment from your own incident history.
