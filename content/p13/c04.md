# Identifying Infrastructure Risks and Assessing Security Posture

:::objectives
- Assess a client's infrastructure and security posture systematically, with tooling and interviews, against a recognised baseline.
- Rate findings by likelihood and impact, separate what blocks your deployment from what you merely report, and write them so the client acts.
- Deliver uncomfortable findings professionally.
:::

:::prereq
[Security and Architecture Questions to Ask](#/p13/c03); Part IV; Part VII chapter 12.
:::

## Why you assess what you did not build

Your system will live inside the client's environment and inherit its weaknesses: a compromised admin account reaches your database; an unpatched bastion is the attacker's path to your pods; backups that were never tested become your outage. You assess the environment because your deployment's security depends on it, because the client often does not know their own posture, and because telling them is part of the value of the role. The assessment is scoped and permissioned in writing, read-only, and framed as helping, not auditing.

## Baselines to assess against

Use a recognised framework so findings are defensible: the CIS Benchmarks for AWS/Azure/GCP (foundational cloud configuration), the AWS Well-Architected security pillar, the client's own policies, and the compliance regime they are subject to. Automated posture tools map configuration to these controls: Prowler, ScoutSuite and Steampipe (open source), AWS Security Hub with CIS and Foundational Security Best Practices standards, Azure Defender for Cloud, GCP Security Command Center. Run them with read-only credentials, store results in the client's environment, and read every high finding yourself before reporting; tools generate noise.

Beyond tooling, assess **operational posture** from interviews and evidence: do they know what they run (inventory)? Does anyone look at alerts? When was the last restore, the last access review, the last incident, the last penetration test, and what changed afterwards? Is there a security owner with time and authority? The answers predict how well the environment will be run after you leave.

## Rating findings

A finding has a **likelihood** (how plausible is exploitation or failure: exposed to the internet with known exploits is high; requires insider with physical access is low) and an **impact** (what happens: full account compromise, regulated data exposure, extended outage, minor information leak). Combine into a severity (critical, high, medium, low) using a simple matrix, consistent across findings. State **exposure** (what is reachable from where), **evidence** (the exact resource, policy, configuration, with IDs and timestamps), **impact in business terms**, and **remediation** (specific, with effort and any dependency).

```text
Finding P-03 · Severity: Critical
Title: Production database security group allows PostgreSQL from the internet
Evidence: sg-0a1b2c3d (vpc-9f8e) ingress 0.0.0.0/0 tcp/5432, attached to db.prod-orders (RDS, publicly accessible = true). Observed 2026-11-01 10:14 UTC via read-only Config query.
Likelihood: High — internet-reachable; credential stuffing and known CVEs for the engine version 13.4 (EOL).
Impact: Full read/write of customer order and payment-reference data (~1.2M customers); regulatory notification likely on compromise.
Remediation: Set publicly accessible = false; restrict SG ingress to application security groups; upgrade engine to a supported version (separate change). Effort: 1 day + change window. Dependency: confirm no legitimate external consumers (reporting tool? ask data team).
Blocks our deployment: Yes — our service will share this database; we cannot go live while it is internet-exposed.
```

## Three categories for the client

1. **Blockers**: conditions under which you will not deploy or go live, because they would make your system unsafe or non-compliant (an internet-exposed shared database; no MFA on the accounts administering your resources; no logging). Agree these as pre-conditions in the plan, with owners and dates. Hold the line; this is the baseline you may not lower (chapter 1).
2. **Risks to your system's operation**: not blockers, but they affect your SLOs and recovery (untested backups, single-person operations, no detection). Mitigate in your design where you can (your own backups to a separate account, your own alerting), record the residual risk and get it accepted.
3. **Observations outside your scope**: weaknesses in systems you do not touch. Report them clearly once, in writing, to the right owner, and move on; you are not their security team, but you would be negligent to stay silent about an open database you happened to see.

## Delivering findings

The posture report lands on people who may be embarrassed, overworked or defensive. Make it land well:

- Preview the top findings with the security or IT lead privately before any wider meeting; no surprises in front of their boss.
- Lead with what is good (there is always something), then the critical items with remediation, then the rest.
- Frame as shared risk to the project, not judgement: "for our deployment to be safe, these three need addressing first; here is how, and here is what we can help with."
- Facts, evidence, dates; no adjectives.
- Offer the quick wins (often an afternoon of IAM clean-up) and the roadmap for the rest.
- Agree owners and dates; put them in the plan; track them like your own tasks.
- Mark the report confidential; it is a map for attackers.

:::fde When the client does not want to hear it
Occasionally a client will decline to fix a blocker and ask you to proceed. Your path: restate the risk and your position in writing; offer the alternative designs that reduce dependence on the weak control (a dedicated account, your own identity boundary); escalate within your organisation; and if the decision is to proceed anyway, ensure the risk acceptance is signed by the client's risk owner and your leadership, and that your system is as isolated from the weakness as you can make it. Do not quietly proceed, and do not quietly refuse.
:::

## Re-assessing

Posture drifts. Re-run the tooling before go-live and at each major milestone; feed continuous checks (Security Hub, Config rules) into the client's operations so the assessment becomes a standing control rather than a one-off report.

## Common mistakes

- Reporting raw tool output: hundreds of findings nobody reads.
- Rating everything high; or rating a public database medium because "it needs a password".
- Findings without evidence or remediation.
- Surprising the IT lead in front of executives.
- Proceeding past a blocker informally.
- Assessing without written permission and scope.

## Exercises

1. Run Prowler or Security Hub against a sandbox account seeded with misconfigurations; write the five most important findings in the structured format, including which block a hypothetical deployment.
2. Build a severity matrix (likelihood × impact) with definitions your team will apply consistently; rate ten tool findings with it and compare ratings with a colleague.
3. Role-play delivering a critical finding to a defensive client IT lead; practise the private preview and the shared-risk framing.
