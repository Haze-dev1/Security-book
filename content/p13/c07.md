# Handling Client Credentials and Sensitive Data

:::objectives
- Receive, store, use and return client credentials and sensitive data without ever becoming the weak link.
- Apply the rules that protect you and the client: least privilege, no copies, no personal devices, audit everything, time-bound access.
- Respond correctly when something goes wrong: a credential exposed, a dataset copied, a laptop lost.
:::

[Key Management and Secrets Management](#/p04/c17); [API Keys and Service Credentials](#/p04/c18); [Data Privacy and Personally Identifiable Information](#/p04/c23); [How Client Data Leaks Actually Happen](#/p04/c24).
:::

## You are a trusted insider

An FDE holds what attackers want: administrative access to client environments, integration credentials for their systems of record, and sometimes their data. Most vendor-related breaches are not sophisticated: a credential in a chat message, a database export on a laptop, a shared password in a document, an access key committed to a repository. The protections are mostly discipline, applied every time, especially when it is inconvenient.

## Principles

1. **Never possess what you do not need.** Ask for the narrowest role for the shortest time. Read-only by default; elevation per task.
2. **Credentials belong to the client.** Issued by them, in their systems, revocable by them, visible to them. You never create credentials the client cannot see or revoke.
3. **No secrets in insecure channels.** Not in email, chat, tickets, documents, screenshots, code, commit messages, or logs. Use their secret manager or a one-time secret-sharing tool they approve, and even then prefer that they enter it directly into the vault themselves.
4. **No copies of data.** Client data stays in client systems. No exports to laptops, personal cloud storage, your organisation's drives or generative AI tools. If analysis requires data, do it in their environment or with anonymised extracts they approve in writing.
5. **Humans use human identities.** Named accounts with MFA; no shared logins; service accounts for services only, with owners.
6. **Everything is logged, and you know it.** Your actions in their environment are recorded; behave as if the log is read daily, because sometimes it is.
7. **Time-bound.** Access expires at the engagement's end or earlier; you confirm removal.

## Receiving access

Preferred patterns in order:

- **Federated identity into their environment**: your organisation's identity provider trusted by theirs (or you added as a guest in their IdP), with their MFA policy, assigned to a role with scoped permissions via their access request process. Nothing to store; revocable centrally.
- **IAM role in their cloud account** trusting your organisation's account or identity provider, with permission boundaries, session duration limits, MFA condition and CloudTrail. You assume it per session; no long-lived keys exist.
- **Named user accounts in their systems** with MFA and least privilege, when federation is not available.
- **Never**: shared accounts, their employees' credentials, long-lived access keys passed over chat, root or global admin.

```hcl
# In the client's account: a role your team assumes, bounded and auditable
resource "aws_iam_role" "vendor_fde" {
  name                 = "vendor-acme-fde"
  max_session_duration = 3600
  assume_role_policy = jsonencode({
    Version = "2012-10-17",
    Statement = [{
      Effect    = "Allow",
      Principal = { AWS = "arn:aws:iam::<VENDOR_ACCOUNT_ID>:root" },   # or an OIDC/SAML provider
      Action    = "sts:AssumeRole",
      Condition = {
        Bool         = { "aws:MultiFactorAuthPresent" = "true" },
        StringEquals = { "sts:ExternalId" = var.external_id },        # prevents confused-deputy misuse
        IpAddress    = { "aws:SourceIp" = var.vendor_egress_cidrs }   # optional: vendor office/VPN ranges
      }
    }]
  })
  permissions_boundary = aws_iam_policy.vendor_boundary.arn   # caps what the role can ever do
  tags = { Vendor = "acme", Expires = "2026-12-31", Owner = "client-it@example" }
}
```

The client reviews the role's permissions; an expiry tag or an automated disable at the contract end removes it; CloudTrail in their log archive records every call with your session name (use `--role-session-name your.name@yourorg` so the trail is attributable).

## Handling integration credentials

Credentials for the client's third-party systems (accounting API keys, payment provider secrets, IdP client secrets) are created by the client or with the client present, placed directly into their secret manager (Secrets Manager, Parameter Store, Vault) in the environment where the application runs, referenced by the application at runtime, rotated on a schedule with the rotation owner named, scoped to the minimum permissions the integration needs, and inventoried in the credentials register with owner and rotation date. You never see the value if the process is right; if you must handle it (vendor portals that show it once), you paste it straight into the vault and close the window, and you record that you handled it.

## Working with sensitive data

- **Development and testing** use synthetic or anonymised data. A production copy "to reproduce the bug" is a breach waiting for a lost laptop; reproduce in their environment with their controls, or anonymise through an approved pipeline.
- **Support access** to production data happens through the application's audited support tooling or through a break-glass procedure with the client's approval, time-bound and logged, never through direct database queries from a laptop unless the runbook says so and the session is recorded.
- **Debugging** uses identifiers in logs and traces, not content; when a payload must be inspected, do it in their environment, and purge afterwards.
- **Screens and screenshots**: blur or avoid personal data in screenshots, demos, tickets and presentations; assume every screenshot is forwarded.
- **Generative AI and external tools**: client data, credentials and often client code may not be pasted into tools the client has not approved; check their policy and yours before using assistants on their material.
- **Devices**: managed, encrypted laptops with screen lock, EDR, no local admin for daily use; nothing client-related on personal devices or phones beyond approved MFA apps and chat.
- **Travel and public spaces**: privacy screens, VPN, no client work on untrusted networks without it, no discussing client incidents in public.

## Audit and attestation

Keep your own log of what access you hold where, and reconcile it monthly against the clients' records; many clients will ask you to attest. At engagement end, produce the access removal confirmation (chapter 12) and verify it yourself: try to assume the role and confirm it fails.

## When something goes wrong

Credential exposed (pasted into chat, committed, shown on a shared screen): rotate immediately through the owner, then investigate use, then tell the client's security contact the same day with facts; the embarrassment of reporting is nothing against the consequence of silence. Data copied where it should not be (an export landed on a laptop): delete securely, record it, and inform the client's data protection contact; they decide whether it is a notifiable event. Laptop lost or stolen: report to both organisations' security teams immediately; remote wipe; rotate every credential it could have cached; assume compromise until proven otherwise. A client employee shares their password with you: decline, use your own access, and mention it to their IT lead as a training matter without blame.

:::danger Reporting is the professional act
Hiding a mistake with client credentials or data turns an incident into misconduct. Every organisation that takes security seriously treats prompt self-reporting as the behaviour it wants. Report first, fix, then write it up.
:::

## Common mistakes

- Accepting a shared admin login "just for the migration".
- Keeping a client's access key in a local AWS profile for months.
- A production dump on a laptop to debug a query.
- Secrets in the ticket that asked for them.
- Pasting client code or data into an unapproved AI assistant.
- Forgetting to confirm access removal at the end.

## Exercises

1. Write the access request you would send a new client: the role, permissions boundary, session duration, MFA, external ID, expiry and audit expectations, with the Terraform they can apply.
2. Audit your own machine and accounts for client credentials and data; remove what should not be there; write down what you found as lessons.
3. Draft the self-report message for an exposed credential: facts, actions taken, what you need from the client, next update time.
