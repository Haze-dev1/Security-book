# Technical Discovery: Understanding a Client's Infrastructure

:::objectives
- Run a structured discovery that maps a client's systems, networks, identity, data, processes and people before designing anything.
- Use documents, interviews and read-only tooling to build an accurate picture quickly, and record what you find in a reusable form.
- Recognise the gaps between what clients believe about their infrastructure and what is true, and plan for them.
:::

:::prereq
[The FDE Role and Decision Authority](#/p13/c01); Parts II, V, VII.
:::

## Why discovery comes first

Every integration surprise, every "we didn't know they had a proxy", every deployment blocked by an unknown firewall team, is a discovery failure. Discovery is the phase where you learn the client's actual environment, not the one in the sales deck, so that the design fits and the estimate holds. Budget it explicitly: one to three weeks for a typical engagement, longer for enterprises, with a written output the client reviews.

## What to discover

```checklist
# Discovery map
## Organisation and people
- Sponsor, decision-makers, budget owner; who signs off on security, architecture, go-live
- Teams you will work with: IT/infrastructure, security, network, data, application owners, procurement, legal; names, roles, response times
- How decisions get made; change advisory process; procurement lead times
- Operating model: who runs production today, hours, on-call, tooling
## Hosting and cloud
- Cloud providers and accounts/subscriptions; organisation structure; landing zone and guardrails (SCPs, policies); approved services and regions
- On-premises data centres; virtualisation; who manages hardware
- Who provisions resources and how (IaC tool, ticket, console); environments that exist
## Network
- Topology: VPCs/VNets, address plans, connectivity (Direct Connect, VPN, ExpressRoute), DNS (internal and external, who owns zones)
- Egress controls: proxies, firewalls, allowlists; inbound: load balancers, WAF, API gateways
- Network segmentation and zones; what can talk to what; how a rule gets changed and how long it takes
## Identity and access
- Workforce IdP (Entra, Okta, Google); protocols supported (OIDC, SAML, SCIM); MFA policy; group structure
- Customer identity (if any); service account policy; privileged access management; access request process
- Who approves access for your team and how long it takes
## Data
- Systems of record and their owners; databases, versions, hosting; data classification scheme
- Personal and regulated data; residency constraints; retention rules; DPO or privacy contact
- Integration interfaces: APIs, files, message buses; documentation quality; test environments available
## Security and compliance
- Policies you must follow: secure coding, encryption, logging, vulnerability SLAs, acceptable tooling
- Compliance regimes in force; audit calendar; evidence they expect
- Security tooling: SIEM, EDR, scanners, secret managers; what they will require of your deployment
- Penetration testing and security review requirements before go-live
## Operations
- Monitoring and logging platforms; alerting and on-call tooling; incident process and severity definitions
- Backup and DR standards; RTO/RPO expectations
- Change management: windows, freezes, approvals, CAB cadence
## Delivery
- Source control, CI/CD platforms you may use or must use; artefact registries
- Developer tooling constraints (managed laptops, allowed software, VDI)
- Existing documentation, diagrams, runbooks; their accuracy
```

## How to discover

**Documents first**: request architecture diagrams, network diagrams, policies, IaC repositories, previous audit reports, and the service catalogue. Read them before interviews so you ask informed questions. Treat every diagram as a hypothesis dated when it was drawn.

**Interviews**: one hour with each key team, structured by the checklist but conversational. Ask open questions ("walk me through how a new service gets to production here") before closed ones. Ask about failures ("what went wrong the last time someone deployed something new?"); the stories reveal the real process. Ask who else you should talk to; the person they name is usually the one who knows.

**Read-only inspection**, with explicit permission and least-privilege, read-only credentials: AWS Config and the account inventory, `aws organizations` structure, VPC and route tables, security groups, IAM roles and policies, CloudTrail presence, DNS zones, certificate inventory, running services and their versions. Tools like Steampipe, Prowler (security posture), and the cloud providers' own well-architected and trusted-advisor reports produce a baseline in hours. Record what tooling you ran, when and with what access.

**Spikes**: for every integration you must build, a small time-boxed proof: can you authenticate to their API from where you will run, call one endpoint, and get expected data? Spikes convert assumptions into facts and are the single best estimate-sharpening investment.

**Observation**: watch an operator deploy, a support agent handle a ticket, a user do the task your system will change. Half the undocumented process lives here.

## What you will find

Patterns that recur across clients:

- **Diagrams lag reality** by a year or more; the firewall rule everyone forgot; the "temporary" server from 2019.
- **The security policy says one thing; the environment does another.** Note both; the gap itself is a finding (chapter 4) and a design constraint (you must meet the policy, not the current state).
- **Identity is more complex than described**: multiple directories, legacy accounts, contractors with standing access, service accounts with passwords in a spreadsheet.
- **Egress is restricted** in ways that break package installation, telemetry and third-party APIs; discover the proxy and allowlist process on day one.
- **Lead times dominate**: a firewall change takes two weeks; a new account, a month; a vendor sandbox, a quarter. Start the requests during discovery, not after design.
- **Hidden owners**: the one engineer who understands the billing integration is on leave or has left; find knowledge concentration early.
- **Shadow IT**: SaaS tools and scripts outside IT's view that your integration depends on.

## Recording discovery

Produce a **discovery report** the client reviews and corrects: the as-is picture (diagrams you drew, inventories, process descriptions), constraints for the design, risks found (with chapter 4's assessment), open questions with owners, and the requests already in flight (access, firewall, sandboxes) with dates. Store it with the project; it becomes the baseline for the specification (Part XII chapter 1) and the architecture decision.

Keep a **contacts and systems register** that outlives the discovery: for every system, who owns it, how to reach them, its environments, how to get access, known quirks. It is the document you consult in every later incident.

:::fde Discovery is also relationship building
The teams you interview are the people who will approve your firewall rules, provision your accounts, and operate your system later. Listening to their constraints and crediting their knowledge during discovery pays back for the whole engagement. The network engineer who tells you about the proxy quirk saved you a week; say so, in front of their manager.
:::

## Common mistakes

- Skipping discovery because the sales team "already covered it".
- Trusting diagrams and policies without inspection.
- Interviewing only the sponsor and the architect, not the operators.
- Not starting long-lead requests during discovery.
- Discovery that produces no written artefact the client confirms.
- Running inspection tooling without explicit written permission and least privilege.

## Exercises

1. Build a discovery question guide for your product's typical client from the checklist; trim it to what fits in three one-hour interviews.
2. Run a read-only inspection of a sandbox cloud account with Prowler or the provider's posture tooling; write the findings section of a discovery report from the output.
3. Draw the as-is architecture of an organisation you know from documents alone, then validate it through two interviews; count the differences.
